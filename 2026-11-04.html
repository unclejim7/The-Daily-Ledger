Midterms · Geopolitics · Wednesday Edition

Senate Flips to the Narrowest of Democratic Majorities as the House Remains Too Close to Call

Control of the Senate shifted to a razor-thin 51-49 Democratic majority overnight, while control of the House remains undetermined this morning, with several districts still too close to project. Divided or narrowly split government now looks like the most likely outcome either way, a result markets appeared to have priced in well before polls closed.

The Gulf situation escalated in a different direction than the Ras Tanura story that has dominated recent editions. Saudi Arabia struck Houthi positions in Dhamar and Hudaydah, Yemen, after the group claimed fresh attacks on Abha International Airport and King Khalid Air Base. Separately, Iranian forces reportedly attempted to shoot down a U.S. aircraft near Qeshm Island on Monday. Aramco, meanwhile, posted a 34% jump in third-quarter profit despite months of attacks on its infrastructure, having shifted more of its export volume to Red Sea routes to reduce its Hormuz exposure.

Markets shrugged off the split election result: the S&P 500 rose 0.3% to 7,647.96, the Dow added 0.2% to 52,185.76, and the Nasdaq gained 0.4% to 25,743.87. Bitcoin extended its climb to roughly $78,700, a fifth straight day of gains, and the 30-year mortgage holds at 7.34% ahead of tomorrow's new Freddie Mac reading.

A Result Without a Mandate

A 51-49 Senate Flip Changes Control Without Changing the Math Much

A two-seat Senate majority leaves almost no room for defections on any contested vote, meaning the practical difference between this outcome and divided government may prove smaller than the headline suggests.

Priced In, Not Ignored

Markets' Calm Reaction Reflects Preparation, Not Indifference

A result this narrow, arriving largely as expected, is exactly the kind of outcome markets are best at pricing in advance — today's modest gains look like confirmation of a thesis, not a surprised reaction to new information.

A Different Front Entirely

Saudi Arabia's Counterstrikes Mark a Shift From Defense to Response

Weeks of absorbing Houthi claims without a public military reply gave way overnight to actual retaliatory strikes in Yemen — a change in posture that may matter more for the conflict's trajectory than anything said or left unsaid about Ras Tanura.

Resilience, by the Numbers

Aramco's Profit Jump Complicates the Simple Reading of This Conflict

A 34% quarterly profit increase amid months of attacks on its own infrastructure suggests the kingdom's energy giant has adapted operationally faster than the headlines around it would suggest — rerouting exports, absorbing costs, and still growing the bottom line.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: A Shift From Absorbing to Answering Marks a New War Cycle Phase

Armstrong Economics has long distinguished between a combatant's defensive and retaliatory phases, arguing the shift from one to the other often marks a conflict's most dangerous inflection point. The site's War Cycle model treats Saudi Arabia's overnight strikes in Yemen as a meaningful break from weeks of largely rhetorical and defensive posture — a sign the kingdom may now be willing to answer force with force rather than simply absorbing it.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Study: Household Drinking Water Is a Major, Largely Unregulated Source of Lithium Exposure

Research examining Tribal communities across the western United States found that household drinking water explained roughly 29% of the variation in people's urinary lithium levels, making tap water a widespread and persistent exposure source. The EPA currently has no enforceable regulatory standard for lithium in drinking water, only a provisional screening level, and researchers caution that the health effects of long-term, low-level lithium exposure remain genuinely uncertain and warrant further study.

Random Fact

A 51-49 Senate split, with the vice president able to break ties only on measures that reach a floor vote without being filibustered, still leaves the chamber's filibuster-bound business requiring 60 votes — meaning a bare majority often changes less about day-to-day legislating than headlines suggest.
