Geopolitics · Iran · Sunday Edition

Trump Says He Rejected Iran's Hormuz Deal Because Tehran Is "Losing So Badly," as Pezeshkian Tells CBS Iran Would Allow UN Inspectors Back In

President Trump confirmed to reporters Saturday that he rejected Iran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, framing Tehran's own urgency as evidence of its weakness: "They want to make a deal where they open the strait immediately because they're losing so badly." He added, "I like making a deal, too, but that deal would not be acceptable," while separately saying Iran had "outsmarted themselves" in how it approached the negotiation. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Tehran had seen Trump's remarks but had not yet received an official response through mediators — leaving the proposal in a kind of diplomatic limbo rather than a clean rejection.

Iran's own posture continued to soften on at least one front: President Pezeshkian told CBS's "Face the Nation" that Iran would allow UN nuclear inspectors back into the country as part of a potential long-term ceasefire deal, calling it "absolutely correct" when asked directly by moderator Margaret Brennan. Pezeshkian tied the offer to the broader collapse of the JCPOA, arguing Iran left that agreement only after a year of unmet European commitments, and said "the process will begin" as soon as Washington accepts Iran's terms. Analysts covering the talks note that an entire week of high-level New York diplomacy produced what one outlet bluntly called "zero progress" toward an actual settlement.

U.S. equity markets are closed for the weekend, leaving Friday's strong close to stand (S&P 500 at 7,743.41, Dow at 51,828.62, Nasdaq at 27,068.72). Bitcoin held steady near $81,250, while gold eased to multi-week lows around $4,280 as the metal continues drifting lower from its post-Fed-hike spike two weeks ago. The 30-year mortgage rate remained at 7.03%, with Freddie Mac's next weekly reading due Thursday.

Framing as Leverage

Calling Iran's Urgency "Losing So Badly" Is a Negotiating Tactic, Not Just an Insult

Publicly characterizing your opponent's willingness to deal as evidence of weakness is a classic pressure tactic aimed at extracting more favorable terms — whether or not it accurately describes Iran's actual position on the ground.

A Rejection With an Asterisk

Awaiting an "Official Response" Keeps the Door from Fully Closing

Araghchi's careful framing — that Tehran has only seen Trump's public remarks, not a formal reply through mediators — suggests both sides are leaving themselves room to walk back today's rejection without losing face domestically.

A Real Concession, With History Attached

Pezeshkian's Inspector Offer Comes Wrapped in a JCPOA Grievance

Framing the inspector concession as contingent on the JCPOA's collapse being the West's fault, not Iran's, lets Pezeshkian offer something substantive while still preserving his domestic narrative of having been wronged first.

A Sobering Week-in-Review

"Zero Progress" Is a Blunt but Fair Assessment

A week that included a UN speech threatening annihilation, a three-hour "very good" meeting, a rejected ceasefire proposal, and a fresh inspector offer produced plenty of headlines but no actual change in the war's underlying status — activity isn't the same as progress.

One Line Item at a Time

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: "Zero Progress" Weeks Are the Norm, Not the Exception, in Protracted Conflicts

Armstrong Economics has long argued that wars lasting beyond their initial acute phase typically settle into extended stretches where diplomatic activity generates headlines without generating actual movement, since neither side has yet reached the point of genuine exhaustion. The site's War Cycle model treats this week's flurry of proposals, rejections, and counteroffers as consistent with a conflict still in that middle stretch — real settlement, on this reading, likely remains months rather than weeks away.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Rheumatoid Arthritis Pain Can Persist Even After Inflammation Improves

New research finds that pain from rheumatoid arthritis can continue even after standard measures of inflammation have improved, with depression, poor sleep, obesity, smoking, and other chronic conditions identified as contributing factors to this persistent pain. Researchers say the findings suggest treatment plans should address these secondary factors directly rather than assuming pain will resolve automatically once inflammation markers come down, pointing toward a more holistic approach to managing the disease.
