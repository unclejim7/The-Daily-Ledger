Geopolitics · Markets · Thursday Edition

Trump Vows to Bomb an Iranian Bridge or Power Plant for Every Ship Iran Hits in Hormuz as Houthis Strike Saudi Tankers and the War’s Price Tag Hits $37.5 Billion

President Trump escalated his rhetoric toward Tehran on Wednesday with his most specific infrastructure threat of the war: “From this point forward, any time the Islamic Republic of Iran shoots at a ship in the Strait of Hormuz, whether it be by Missile, Rocket, Drone, or any other device or weapon, the United States will bomb and destroy ONE BRIDGE OR POWER PLANT, including those located next to, or in, the Capital City of Tehran,” he wrote on Truth Social. It is a marked sharpening from his earlier, vaguer threats to level Iran’s “civilization” — this one reads like a standing order. Iran answered fast: an IRGC Aerospace Force commander warned that hitting Iranian power stations would guarantee “the power outage of the allies and hosts of the child killers,” while Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called Iran’s doctrine “eye for an eye” and warned that any nation assisting U.S. strikes would be treated as a legitimate target. Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said flatly that the Strait “will not return to its pre-war status.” Targeting civilian infrastructure such as power plants and bridges not directly tied to military operations sits in a legal gray zone that rights groups say could constitute a war crime under the Rome Statute, though the U.S. is not an ICC member.

Overnight, U.S. Central Command carried out a 12th consecutive night of strikes on Iran, and the war gained a second front: Yemen’s Houthi rebels said they struck two Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea early Thursday, days after declaring a naval blockade of the kingdom. The UK Maritime Trade Operations center confirmed a tanker was hit southwest of Al Shuqaiq on Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea coast; the Institute for the Study of War says at least seven vessels have already diverted away from the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Thursday the U.S. would hold Iran responsible for the Houthi attacks, accusing Tehran of “suckering” its Yemeni allies into the fight. The timing matters: Saudi Arabia has been routing crude through Red Sea terminals as a workaround since the Hormuz blockade shut its Gulf export routes, and that workaround is now directly under fire.

The financial toll came into sharper focus this week, too. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told the Senate Appropriations Committee Tuesday that the war has cost the U.S. $37.5 billion so far — up from the roughly $25–30 billion figures the Pentagon had cited as recently as April — and that the administration wants another $67 billion in supplemental funding to keep operations going through the fiscal year. Lawmakers from both parties pushed back hard; Sen. Patty Murray called the request’s math incoherent, and NBC News has reported the Pentagon’s internal all-in estimate, including base repairs, runs as high as $80–100 billion.

Markets are still mostly trading around the war rather than through it, but oil is the transmission mechanism to watch: Brent settled Wednesday at $94.07, up 3.4%, and pushed past $98 in Thursday trading on the Houthi escalation, while WTI climbed above $90 for the first time in six weeks. That, combined with mixed results from Alphabet and Tesla after Wednesday’s close, has U.S. futures pointed lower to start Thursday.

Alphabet Beats but Raises Capex to $205B; Tesla Posts Its First Negative Free Cash Flow Quarter in Two Years

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Hike Odds Climb Again

Fed Funds Futures Now Price Roughly 36% Odds of a July 29 Hike as the Oil Shock Complicates Warsh’s Path

The renewed spike in crude — WTI above $90, Brent near $98 — has pushed the market-implied odds of a quarter-point hike at next Wednesday’s FOMC meeting to roughly 36%, up from the high-teens just a few weeks ago, according to Thursday morning fed funds futures pricing. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack has been among the most vocal hawks, echoing Chair Kevin Warsh’s own line from the ECB’s Sintra forum that “prices are too high.” Warsh, who has held rates at 3.50%–3.75% for four straight meetings and became the first chair in memory to withhold his own dot from the June projections, faces a genuinely live decision now: a hold remains the base case, but the gap between hold and hike has narrowed meaningfully as the war’s energy shock threatens to undo the disinflationary progress from June’s cooler CPI and PPI prints. The European Central Bank, by contrast, is expected to hold its own rate steady at 2.25% later Thursday.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Small Upgrades to Sleep, Diet, and Exercise Could Cut Heart Risk by 10%, Research Shows

A widely-cited 2026 cardiovascular study out of the University of Sydney found that sleeping just 11 more minutes a night, adding roughly five extra minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day, and eating an additional quarter-cup of vegetables was associated with a 10% reduction in heart attacks, strokes, and other major cardiac events over eight years of follow-up. The finding, highlighted this week by Harvard Health, is notable less for the size of the individual changes than for how small they are — a useful reminder on a week when the news cycle has been dominated by things well outside any one person’s control. None of the three levers requires a gym membership or a diet overhaul; the researchers’ point is that the marginal, easily-ignored habits compound the same way the harmful ones do.

Editor’s Dispatch

Edition 58. Twelve nights in, and the war just acquired a mechanism — a bridge or a power plant, automatically, every time a ship gets hit. There’s something almost bureaucratic about turning escalation into a standing policy like that, and I don’t think the bureaucratic framing makes it feel smaller so much as it makes it feel more permanent. The Houthis picking a fight with Saudi Arabia is the kind of development that would have been the whole newspaper eighteen months ago; today it’s a paragraph next to Alphabet’s capex guidance. Hegseth’s $37.5 billion number is worth sitting with for a second — that’s not an abstraction, it’s a number that shows up somewhere on a balance sheet the country will be looking at for years. The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

Yanbu, the Red Sea port Saudi Arabia has leaned on to reroute crude exports since the Hormuz blockade began, sits at the western end of the Petroline — a 750-mile pipeline built in the 1980s specifically as an alternative to the Strait of Hormuz after Saudi Arabia watched Iran and Iraq trade tanker attacks during the 1980s “Tanker War.” The pipeline was designed as insurance against exactly the scenario playing out now, four decades later, with the insurance itself under direct attack.

Word of the Day
brinkmanship

(noun, diplomatic and political strategy, mid-20th century) — the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the very edge of open conflict in order to force an opponent to back down first. Coined during the Cold War to describe nuclear-era diplomacy, the term applies neatly to Wednesday’s bridge-and-power-plant ultimatum: a threat calibrated to be credible enough to deter, without — its author would presumably argue — actually being carried out.
