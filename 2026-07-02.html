Federal Reserve · Labor Market · Thursday Edition

June Jobs Report Whiffs at Just 57,000 as Yields Fall and Wall Street Shrugs Off Rate-Hike Fears Into the Holiday

The Bureau of Labor Statistics delivered the week’s most consequential data point Thursday morning, and it landed well short of expectations. U.S. nonfarm payrolls rose by a seasonally adjusted 57,000 in June, far below the 115,000 Dow Jones consensus forecast and a sharp step down from May’s 129,000. Yet the unemployment rate, drawn from a separate household survey, unexpectedly fell to 4.2% from 4.3%, a combination that left economists parsing a labor market that is cooling on the hiring side while showing no sign of the layoffs that would typically accompany a downturn. The report was moved up a day from its usual first-Friday slot because markets are closed Friday for Independence Day.

Bond markets moved quickly on the news. The 2-year Treasury yield, most sensitive to near-term Fed expectations, dropped more than 5 basis points to 4.108% as traders further discounted the odds of a July rate hike. The 10-year yield eased about a basis point to roughly 4.467%. “Overall, this morning’s data makes it difficult to envision a path toward a July Fed hike even if there is upside in the inflation data yet to be realized,” said Ian Lyngen, head of U.S. rates strategy at BMO Capital Markets. The report followed Wednesday’s soft ADP print of 98,000 private-sector jobs and arrived one day after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, appearing at the ECB’s Sintra forum in Portugal alongside Christine Lagarde, Andrew Bailey, and Tiff Macklem, declined to preview the Fed’s July decision while reaffirming that “we’ve seen that prices are too high.” Markets had been pricing a roughly 29% chance of a July hike heading into Thursday; that probability slipped further after the payrolls miss.

Equities opened higher on the report before gains moderated through the morning. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, which closed Wednesday at a second straight record of 52,319.20, added as much as 246 points at the open before settling to a gain of roughly 124 points, or 0.24%, near 52,443. The S&P 500 was little changed, up about 0.12% to 7,508.51, while the Nasdaq Composite slipped 0.15% as semiconductor stocks — Micron, AMD, Intel, and Sandisk all fell sharply on Wednesday — continued to lag the broader market's rotation into more economically sensitive names. Tesla was a bright spot, reporting second-quarter deliveries of 480,126 vehicles, comfortably ahead of the 406,600 analysts had forecast and up from 384,000 a year earlier.

The report caps a holiday-shortened week that will end with markets closed Friday for the Fourth of July. With Wall Street coming off its best quarter since 2020 and the labor market sending mixed signals, the next major catalyst is the July 28–29 FOMC meeting, where Warsh’s Fed will have to reconcile a cooling jobs market against inflation that remains, in the chair’s own words, too high.

Labor Market

Nonfarm Payrolls Rise Just 57,000 as Unemployment Rate Unexpectedly Falls to 4.2%

June’s establishment survey showed the smallest payroll gain in months, well below the 115,000 consensus and a step down from May’s revised 129,000. Yet the household survey told a different story: the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% from 4.3%, defying expectations it would hold steady. The divergence has economists split on whether June marks the start of a genuine slowdown or a data-collection quirk tied to the shortened survey week around the World Cup and Memorial Day timing that inflated May’s figures.

Tesla Delivers 480,126 Vehicles in Q2, Comfortably Beating the 406,600 Wall Street Expected

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Sintra Aftermath

Warsh's "Prices Are Too High" Framing Collides With a Soft Jobs Report — Which Signal Wins?

Wednesday's Sintra remarks and Thursday's payrolls miss now sit in tension at the center of Fed policy debate. Warsh told the ECB Forum panel that "we've all looked around, and we've seen that prices are too high," reiterating that price stability remains his primary objective even as he expressed growing openness to the idea that AI could prove disinflationary over time. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, at the same conference, struck a more explicitly hawkish note earlier in the week. But Thursday's data cuts the other way: a 57,000 payroll gain against a 115,000 estimate is the kind of soft print that historically pulls a central bank toward patience, not tightening. Markets are currently pricing roughly a 65% chance of at least a quarter-point hike by the September meeting, according to the CME FedWatch tool, though that probability could shift meaningfully once the jobs data is fully digested. The next FOMC decision lands July 28–29, and Thursday's report ensures that meeting arrives with a genuinely two-sided debate rather than a hawkish consensus.

Technical Picture — Thursday

Bitcoin Bounces Off Support Near $58,000 as Whales Accumulate 270,000 BTC in Two Weeks

Bitcoin opened July in the green, climbing roughly 2.7% to reclaim ground above $59,800 after closing June down approximately 20.5% — its steepest monthly decline of the current cycle and one that drove spot Bitcoin ETFs to their worst month on record, with $4.5 billion in net outflows. CryptoQuant data shows BTC whales have aggressively accumulated more than 270,000 BTC over the past two weeks even as retail sentiment remains mired in Extreme Fear. Peter Schiff, chief economist at Europac, has warned the $58,000 level must hold to avoid a slide toward $50,000, while more constructive analysts point to July's historically strong seasonality — Bitcoin has closed the month in the green in 9 of the last 13 years, averaging a 7.25% gain. The next major catalyst is whether ETF flows turn net-positive; a reversal there, paired with a benign July 28–29 FOMC meeting, is the bull case for a run back toward $65,000–$70,000 by month's end.

NerdWallet's 30-Year Rate Ticks Up to 6.40% APR as Freddie Mac's Weekly Survey Lands Later Today

Mortgage rates drifted modestly higher to open July. NerdWallet's APR-based 30-year fixed rate rose 4 basis points Thursday morning to 6.40%, up 12 basis points from a week ago but still 14 basis points below year-ago levels. Bankrate's daily average put the 30-year fixed at 6.51%, while Optimal Blue data via Fortune showed the conforming 30-year rate at 6.452%, up roughly 3 basis points from the prior day. Freddie Mac's benchmark weekly Primary Mortgage Market Survey — last reported at 6.49% for the week ending June 25 — is due for its regular Thursday release, the first reading to fully capture this week's jobs-driven yield moves. Bankrate's Mortgage Rate Variability Index reads just 2 out of 10, signaling unusually little difference between lender offers right now. Housing economists at the MBA and Fannie Mae continue to project 30-year rates holding in the mid-6% range through year-end regardless of whether the Fed moves in July.

Tax & Accounting

Tens of Millions of Taxpayers May Qualify for COVID-Era Penalty Refunds — But Must File by July 10

The National Taxpayer Advocate is flagging a July 10, 2026 deadline for taxpayers to claim refunds or abatements of penalties and interest the IRS assessed during the roughly 3.5-year COVID-19 federal disaster period, following a court decision in the Kwong case that calls into question the agency's authority to assess those charges. Relief is not automatic: most affected taxpayers — individuals, small businesses, estates, and trusts among them — must file Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement, by the deadline to preserve their rights. Because the underlying legal question remains in litigation, the Advocate is also recommending protective claims for taxpayers whose situations are less clear-cut; such claims don't require an exact dollar figure but must describe the contingencies affecting the claim. Taxpayers with open examinations or appeals may have additional time. This is separate from last week's July 6 deadline for retroactive research-and-experimental expense elections under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — both dates are worth flagging for anyone who filed returns during the pandemic years.

Dansby Swanson Hits Three Homers, Drives In 8 as Cubs Demolish Padres 23-3 to Complete Sweep

The Cubs closed out a three-game sweep of San Diego in historic fashion Wednesday at Wrigley Field, pounding out 17 hits and tying the franchise record with eight home runs in a single game. Dansby Swanson delivered the best offensive game of his career — three home runs, including a grand slam off position player Rodolfo Durán in the eighth, and a career-high eight RBI, the most by a Cub since Sammy Sosa's nine in 2002. It was his first career three-homer game and second straight multi-homer effort, making him the first Cubs shortstop since 1900 to do that in consecutive games. His 26 RBI over his last 10 games ties Joe DiMaggio (1939), Jimmie Foxx (1933), Lou Gehrig (1930/31), and Mel Ott (1929) for the most in a 10-game span since RBI became official in 1920. Michael Conforto added two homers of his own. "We never have baseball figured out," said manager Craig Counsell. "I think this tells you that." Chicago improved to 49-38, five games behind Milwaukee in the NL Central, having won nine of its last 10. The Cardinals arrive at Wrigley for a holiday-weekend series starting Friday.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

That Fan in Your Window Might Be Working Against You — Here's When Cooling Advice Flips

With today marking the final stretch of Chicagoland's Extreme Heat Warning, it's worth revisiting a piece of guidance that surprises a lot of people: the CDC advises against running electric fans once indoor temperatures climb above 90°F, because moving hot air across the skin can actually raise core body temperature rather than lower it. The World Health Organization sets its own threshold even higher, at 104°F. Fans work by speeding evaporative cooling from sweat, which is genuinely effective in moderate heat — but once ambient air exceeds skin temperature, that same airflow just circulates heat instead of removing it. The practical takeaway for the rest of today's high: a fan pointed at you in a hot, un-air-conditioned room past mid-morning is doing less than you think, and a brief trip somewhere air-conditioned — a library, a store, a cooling center — does more to actually lower body temperature than hours in front of a fan. Relief arrives on its own tomorrow as Friday's rain moves in and highs ease into the 80s.

Random Fact

Dansby Swanson's three-homer game Wednesday made him just the sixth player in modern MLB history (since RBI became an official statistic in 1920) to record 26 or more RBI in any 10-game span — joining a quartet of Hall of Famers: Joe DiMaggio (1939), Jimmie Foxx (1933), and Lou Gehrig, who did it in both 1930 and 1931. Two months ago Swanson was hitting .175. Baseball's counting stats reward exactly this kind of streak: a cold six weeks disappears into irrelevance against ten scorching games, which is part of why box scores from June feel like ancient history by the All-Star break.
