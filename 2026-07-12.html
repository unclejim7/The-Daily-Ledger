Geopolitics · Energy · Weekend Edition

Iran Closes the Strait of Hormuz and Strikes Five Gulf Nations as the US Answers With a Third Round of Airstrikes

The fragile, three-week-old collapse of the June ceasefire tipped into its most dangerous weekend yet overnight. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps opened fire on the Cyprus-flagged container ship GFS Galaxy early Sunday after accusing it of sailing an “unauthorized route” through the Strait of Hormuz, leaving the vessel disabled by fire and engine-room damage roughly 17 kilometers east of Oman with one crew member missing; the IRGC later said it struck and stopped a second vessel as well. In response, U.S. Central Command carried out its third round of strikes on Iran in a week, hitting roughly 140 military sites — missile and drone facilities, naval assets, ammunition depots, communications networks and coastal radar — which CENTCOM described as holding Iranian forces accountable for the tanker attack. The IRGC answered by declaring the strait “closed until further notice and until the end of American interventions in this region,” even as CENTCOM insisted commercial vessels continue to transit the waterway and posted a pointed “fact check” on social media: “Iran does not control the Strait of Hormuz. It remains an international waterway.”

Tehran then widened its retaliation dramatically, launching missile and drone strikes on U.S.-linked military sites across five countries. The IRGC said it destroyed a command-and-control center and MQ-9 drone hangars at Jordan’s Prince Hassan air base, hit a Patriot air-defense system and radar site in Kuwait, and struck a jet maintenance facility and command post in Qatar; explosions lit up the sky over Doha as residents received emergency alerts to shelter in place. Qatar’s interior ministry said three people, including a child, were wounded by falling shrapnel from interceptions. Jordan reported three missiles landed on its soil with only minor damage; Bahrain sounded air-raid sirens and activated its Fifth Fleet-area defenses; the UAE said its systems engaged incoming missiles and drones before later clarifying the threats were intercepted outside its borders; and Oman, which had hosted Iran’s foreign minister for Hormuz talks just a day earlier, called the strikes on its Musandam region a “dismaying” and “irresponsible” violation of its sovereignty. Iran’s top negotiator, parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, framed the barrage as retribution for broken promises, posting an image of Article 5 of the Islamabad memorandum on the Strait of Hormuz and warning that “the era of one-sided deals is OVER” and that countries must “keep your word or pay the price.”

Wall Street will not get its first live read on any of this until Sunday evening, when electronic futures trading resumes; the physical oil market and U.S. equities have been closed since Friday’s close, when the S&P 500 finished at 7,575.39 and WTI crude settled at $71.41 a barrel, up about 3.5% on the week on the earlier flare-up alone. Analysts had already flagged that a genuine, sustained Hormuz closure — as opposed to a rhetorical one — could add $15–$20 a barrel within a week if shipping insurers and traders conclude the threat is real rather than posturing; UN shipping authorities have urged countries to reject Iran’s sovereignty claim over the waterway outright. Whether Sunday night’s open treats this as the former or the latter will set the tone for a week that already includes Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s first congressional testimony and June CPI.

Diplomacy has not entirely gone dark despite the barrage: Iranian and Omani officials met again in Muscat on Saturday to discuss “appropriate mechanisms for the safe passage of ships” under Article 5 of the memorandum, and a Qatari delegation was reportedly present for legal and technical talks that both sides agreed to continue. But the pattern of the past five days — strike, retaliate, meet, strike again — is now well-established, and UANI analysts argue Iran has breached nearly every major pillar of the June agreement: the ceasefire itself, freedom of navigation, and cooperation on the nuclear file. New Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, who has not appeared in public or released any image or recording since his father’s death, issued a written vow of vengeance Saturday — a signal, most regional analysts believe, that this weekend’s escalation was sanctioned at the very top of a leadership structure still consolidating control.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Setting Up the Week

Warsh Heads to Capitol Hill Tuesday With June CPI Landing 90 Minutes Before He Sits Down — Now With a Fresh Oil Shock to Explain

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh testifies before the House Financial Services Committee Tuesday at 10 a.m. in his first semiannual monetary policy appearance before Congress, followed by the Senate Banking Committee Wednesday. The timing was already awkward: June CPI data lands just 90 minutes before the House hearing begins, making it nearly impossible for Warsh to dodge questions about the inflation picture. Economists had penciled in some relief, with headline CPI forecast to ease toward 3.9% and core holding near 2.9%. Sunday’s renewed Hormuz closure complicates that math considerably — not for June’s already-recorded data, but for the July and August prints that will follow if oil prices reprice higher on a sustained disruption. Warsh has repeatedly declined to offer forward guidance since taking the gavel in May, and the Fed’s own monetary policy report released Friday reiterated that core PCE inflation remains well above the 2% target at 3.4% as of May. Futures markets already price better-than-75% odds of at least one 2026 hike; a real Hormuz shock would only reinforce that lean heading into September.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Hot Summer Nights Are Quietly Wrecking Sleep, New Research Shows — Worth Knowing Before This Week’s Heat Wave Arrives

A new analysis published in the journal Sleep finds that warm nights are reshaping how well people rest on a global scale: on nights when temperatures stay at or above 81°F, roughly 9,300 additional people out of every 100,000 get less than six hours of sleep. Researchers describe rising nighttime heat as a public-health issue “hiding in plain sight,” since heat suppresses the normal drop in core body temperature that signals the body to enter deeper sleep stages. With Chicagoland’s next stretch of 90-degree days and warm, humid nights arriving Monday through Wednesday, the practical countermeasures are simple: keep bedrooms dark and as cool as possible, take a cool shower before bed to help trigger that temperature drop, skip evening alcohol since it raises body temperature and fragments sleep, and aim a fan at a bowl of ice if air conditioning isn’t available.

The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

The Strait of Hormuz is only 21 miles wide at its narrowest point, with shipping lanes in each direction just two miles across — yet in ordinary times it carries roughly one-fifth of all oil and gas traded by sea worldwide, some 20 million barrels a day. That geography is precisely why it has changed hands rhetorically, if not physically, so many times since February: a chokepoint that narrow means a handful of missile boats and shore batteries can credibly threaten global energy flows without ever needing to control a single mile of open ocean.

Word of the Day
brinkmanship

(noun, coined in American political commentary, 1950s) — the practice of pushing a dangerous situation to the verge of disaster in order to achieve the most advantageous outcome, calculating that the other side will back down first. Coined during the Cold War to describe nuclear-era diplomacy, it fits the strike-retaliate-negotiate-strike pattern both Washington and Tehran have now repeated for five straight days over the Strait of Hormuz — each side betting the other blinks before the shipping lane actually stays shut.
