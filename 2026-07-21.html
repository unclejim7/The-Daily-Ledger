Geopolitics · Markets · Tuesday Edition

U.S. Strikes Iran for a 10th Consecutive Night as Wall Street Looks Past the War Toward a Marquee Earnings Week

U.S. Central Command carried out its tenth straight night of strikes on Iran beginning Monday at 4 p.m. ET, hitting what CENTCOM described as Iranian military command centers, maritime capabilities, missile and drone launch sites, and air defense systems, with the operation completed by 9 p.m. ET. Iranian state and semi-official media reported explosions across a wide swath of the country overnight and into Tuesday morning — Bandar Abbas, Sirik Island, Qeshm, Shiraz, Isfahan, Bandar Lengeh, Chabahar, and Konarak among them — with air defense systems activated near the Bushehr nuclear power plant and a wave of strikes reported on the IRGC’s Imam Ali base in Chabahar. Iranian officials said no attack had actually been carried out on Isfahan, home to Iran’s largest nuclear facility, attributing explosions there to air defenses firing rather than impacts.

Iran answered in kind. The IRGC claimed retaliatory strikes on U.S.-linked HIMARS systems, radar, satellite receivers, a telecommunications center, and an MQ-9 drone hangar across multiple sites in Kuwait, and said it destroyed radar and air defense systems in Bahrain. Two Greek-owned tankers were struck in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, and the UK Maritime Trade Operations center reported a separate tanker hit off Oman’s port of Limah, forcing its crew to abandon ship. Early Tuesday, the IRGC claimed two additional tankers were disabled attempting to use what it called an “unsafe southern route” through the strait. Jordan’s military said it intercepted five Iranian drones overnight and into Tuesday morning. President Trump, writing on Truth Social, warned Iran that “every time Iran kills an American Soldier, they will pay for that killing many times over,” and an Israeli security source told i24NEWS that further U.S. escalation is expected in the coming days.

The American death toll held at 18 Tuesday, following a brutal weekend in which two soldiers, 1st Lt. Tyler James Feehan, 25, and Pvt. Isabella Gonzales, 19, were killed in Iran’s missile strike on Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base, and a third service member died in northern Iraq during a controlled detonation of an unexploded Iranian drone. A dignified transfer at Dover Air Force Base, initially expected Tuesday, has been postponed with no new date set, the White House said. Separately, the Pentagon pushed back Monday on a New York Times report alleging it had slow-walked disclosure of troop injuries, saying nearly 100 service members have been hurt since July 7 and 96% have already returned to duty — the vast majority described as minor concussions.

Wall Street, for the second straight session, largely traded through the war. Monday’s cash close was soft — the S&P 500 fell 0.19% to 7,443.28, the Nasdaq slipped 0.05% to 25,508.07, and the Dow dropped 307 points (-0.59%) to 51,839.26 as Apple’s more-than-2% decline weighed on the blue chips — but Tuesday futures pointed higher as chip stocks staged a fresh rebound and South Korea’s KOSPI jumped more than 3.5% overnight. Investors are increasingly looking past the daily strike-and-retaliate rhythm toward this week’s earnings wall, with 3M and General Motors both beating estimates Tuesday morning and Alphabet, Tesla, Intel, and IBM still to come.

Earnings Week

3M and GM Kick Off a Marquee Earnings Week With Beats as Alphabet, Tesla, Intel and IBM Loom

3M jumped more than 5% premarket after topping second-quarter estimates, while General Motors beat on the bottom line with adjusted earnings of $3.57 per share on $48.03 billion in revenue, versus $3.20 and $47.01 billion expected, and raised its full-year EBIT guidance. Alphabet, Intel, IBM, and Tesla all report later this week, and the results are expected to shape whether the AI trade’s recent wobble was a pause or the start of something more durable.

Crude remains up more than 25% year-over-year and roughly 12% over the past month as the reimposed Hormuz blockade and repeated strikes on Gulf infrastructure keep a war premium embedded in the price, even as gains have moderated from the acute spikes of the past week.

Fed Watch

Cleveland Fed’s Hammack Joins the Hawks as December Hike Odds Climb to 80%

Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack became the latest policymaker to suggest further rate hikes may be needed to contain persistent inflation, and futures markets have responded: traders now price an 80% probability of a December rate hike, up from 73% a week ago. The shift builds on Chair Kevin Warsh’s June dot plot, which dropped the Fed’s last projected 2026 rate cut entirely, and comes even as June’s cooler CPI and PPI readings had briefly given markets room to expect a longer hold.

Policy Path

A Hawkish Chorus Builds as War-Driven Oil Complicates the Case for a July Hold

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s committee still enters the July 28–29 meeting with a hold as the overwhelming base case, but the tone around the edges has turned more hawkish by the day. Hammack’s comments Monday follow Warsh’s own repeated insistence at the ECB’s Sintra forum that “prices are too high,” and the June dot plot already showed nine of 18 officials penciling in at least one 2026 hike. The complicating variable is the same one that has shadowed every Fed data point since the war reignited on July 7: crude oil is up double digits over the past month, and whatever relief June’s cooler CPI and PPI offered could prove short-lived if July’s energy-driven inflation shows up in the data the Fed sees before its next decision.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Why Wildfire Smoke Is Riskier Than It Looks — and What Actually Cuts Your Exposure

With Chicagoland's air quality still working its way down from Monday's unhealthy readings, physicians note that wildfire smoke's danger isn't just the burning smell — it's the fine particulate matter, small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, that drives measurable increases in heart attacks, stroke, and respiratory illness even during a single bad-air episode. Research following recent smoke events has linked exposure to nearly a 20% jump in emergency-room visits for asthma, along with more cardiovascular-related visits on smoky days. The protective steps that actually move the needle are simple: stay inside with windows closed and air conditioning recirculating rather than pulling in outside air, check a real-time AQI reading before heading out rather than judging by how hazy it looks, and reach for a properly fitted N95 mask if you have to be outside for any length of time. Children, older adults, pregnant women, and anyone with asthma, COPD, or heart disease face the highest risk and should keep outdoor time minimal until the air clears.

Random Fact

The Strait of Hormuz, at its narrowest point, is only about 21 miles wide, with the actual shipping lanes on each side just two miles across — separated by a two-mile buffer zone. Despite that narrowness, roughly a fifth of the world's total oil consumption has historically passed through it, which is why a single disabled tanker or a naval blockade announcement can move global crude prices within minutes, long before anyone confirms how much traffic has actually been disrupted.

Word of the Day
chokepoint

(noun, military and geographic strategy) — a narrow passage through which forces, traffic, or trade must funnel, making it disproportionately valuable to control or disrupt. The term originated in naval strategy to describe straits and canals where a numerically inferior force could still exert outsized influence simply by controlling the passage. The Strait of Hormuz is the textbook modern example: Iran doesn't need to defeat the U.S. Navy to disrupt global energy markets, it only needs to make transit through a 21-mile-wide chokepoint unpredictable.
