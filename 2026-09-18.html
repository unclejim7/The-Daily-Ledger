Geopolitics · Markets · Friday Edition

Hundreds of Thousands Rally in Tehran Behind a Trump-in-Crosshairs Banner as Wall Street Notches Its Best Session Since the Fed Hike

Iran staged its biggest show of public defiance since the war began Friday, as hundreds of thousands rallied across Tehran in a state-organized march dubbed "Janfada," or "Sacrifice for Iran." Women affiliated with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps carried rifles beneath a banner depicting President Trump in crosshairs alongside the words "We will kill," while Tehran's Guard chief, Gen. Hassan Hassanzadeh, told state television that more than 600,000 people had already registered for paramilitary training, with over a million expected to eventually take part. It was the largest such demonstration since the U.S. and Israel struck Iran on February 28.

The timing is difficult to read as coincidental. The rally lands just five days before Tuesday's planned meeting between Trump and six Gulf leaders on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, and only two days after Trump himself told Axios he has a "big decision" pending on whether to "annihilate" the Iranian regime. Both sides, in other words, spent the same week escalating their public rhetoric rather than dialing it back — not an encouraging signal for a summit meant to chart the war's postwar course.

Markets, at least, found reasons for relief. Thursday delivered Wall Street's best session since Wednesday's rate hike: the Dow climbed 316.14 points (+0.6%) to 51,778.04, the S&P 500 rose 1.1% to 7,637.76, and the Nasdaq jumped 1.7% to 26,418.30, as falling oil prices and easing bond-market pressure helped reverse much of the prior day's Fed-driven selloff. Brent crude eased to $104.82 and WTI to $101.91, both down modestly on the day even as crude remains up sharply for the month.

Bitcoin recovered some lost ground, rebounding toward $78,000 after Wednesday's slide below $76,000, while gold gave back part of its post-hike surge, easing to roughly $4,320 from Wednesday's $4,368.91 close. Freddie Mac's 30-year mortgage rate held at 6.95%, still its highest level since January 2025, with no new weekly reading due until next Thursday.

Two Capitals, Two Signals

Tehran and Washington Both Escalated the Same Week

A "big decision" on annihilation from Trump and a rifle-carrying rally under a crosshairs banner from Tehran, delivered within 48 hours of each other, look less like unrelated news items than two capitals talking past each other in the loudest available language, days before they're due to sit near the same table.

Numbers Worth Watching

600,000 Registered Is a Message, Not Just a Headcount

Whether or not the figure holds up to scrutiny, a regime choosing to publicize mass paramilitary registration numbers this specific and this large is itself a form of signaling, aimed as much at Tuesday's Gulf summit as at any domestic audience.

A Reversal, Not a Resolution

Thursday's Rally Undoes the Selloff, Not the Underlying Story

A single strong session driven by falling oil and calming yields is a relief, not a verdict; the same hawkish Fed framing and war-risk premium that drove Wednesday's selloff are both still very much in place heading into next week.

A Snapback, Not a Trend Change

Bitcoin's Bounce Comes After Six Rough Weeks

Blockade Economics

The Rally Can't Paper Over the Naval Blockade's Toll

Iran's own officials have acknowledged the naval blockade is worsening the domestic economy even as the government stages shows of defiance, a tension between public messaging and private hardship that tends to widen the longer a war like this drags on.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Mass Mobilization Displays Signal Domestic Pressure, Not Strength

Armstrong Economics has repeatedly argued that regimes stage large public mobilization displays precisely when internal pressure is building, using visible shows of unity to manage domestic morale rather than to project genuine external strength. A state-organized rally emphasizing huge registration numbers, arriving the same week Iran's own blockade-strained economy remains under visible stress, fits that reading — the site's War Cycle model treats this kind of theater as more diagnostic of strain than of resolve.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Any Daily Step Count Above 2,200 Linked to Lower Death and Cardiovascular Risk

A new analysis finds that taking more steps each day is associated with a lower risk of both death and cardiovascular disease, with benefits appearing at surprisingly modest activity levels — any amount of daily walking above roughly 2,200 steps showed a measurable reduction in risk, well below the commonly cited 10,000-step benchmark. Researchers say the findings should encourage a lower-barrier framing of physical activity guidance, since meaningful benefit doesn't require hitting an intimidating daily target.
