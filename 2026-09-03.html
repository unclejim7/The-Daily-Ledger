Geopolitics · Markets · Thursday Edition

Iran Strikes Jordan, Bahrain, and Kuwait in Retaliation for US Attacks That Killed 11, as Tehran Claims Two Tankers Hit Mines Washington Says Don't Exist

Iran carried out its most geographically sweeping retaliation of the entire war Tuesday night, launching missiles and drones not just at U.S. forces in the immediate Gulf but at targets in Jordan, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Iraq's Kurdistan region simultaneously. Jordan's military said 13 Iranian missiles entered its airspace, with 10 intercepted and three landing in remote areas without casualties; the IRGC said it specifically targeted a hangar housing American RQ-4 and MQ-9 drones at Prince Hassan Airbase. Bahrain's air defenses intercepted incoming drones, Kuwait's military responded to hostile missiles and drones of its own, and the IRGC claimed to have destroyed a maintenance center and equipment at a U.S. base in Iraqi Kurdistan. The response followed U.S. strikes that killed 11 people in Iran, part of the broader "tanker for tanker" campaign detailed in this space Wednesday.

The two sides also spent the day disputing a more specific, and in its way more fundamental, question: whether the mines Trump insisted last week had been "completely removed or detonated" from the strait ever stopped being a problem at all. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said two tankers struck naval mines while attempting to transit Hormuz, a claim the U.S. flatly disputes. It's the same underlying disagreement that's defined this war for weeks — two governments offering irreconcilable accounts of the same stretch of water — now playing out over an incident with actual physical evidence, or the lack of it, rather than just competing rhetoric.

Markets, remarkably, took all of this in stride. The S&P 500 rose 0.5% Thursday, the Nasdaq gained 0.6%, and the Dow jumped 401 points, or 0.8%, even as oil climbed on the fresh round of attacks — WTI up 2.1% to $92.94, Brent up 1.9% to $97.45. Treasury yields actually eased, with the 10-year slipping back to around 4.75% after Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he'd be "inclined to support" holding rates steady barring any inflation surprises, a notably more moderate voice than Warsh's hawkish framing from last week's Jackson Hole remarks.

Bitcoin ticked higher to around $77,900 despite the oil-driven risk backdrop, while gold climbed nearly 1% to about $4,428 an ounce, both assets seemingly more responsive to the softer yield picture than to a war that just expanded into four additional countries overnight. Wednesday's cash session had closed essentially flat (S&P 500 at 7,666.60, Nasdaq +0.45% to 26,217.83, Dow +0.56% to 53,061.95) ahead of Thursday's rally.

The Scope

Four Countries Struck in a Single Night

Jordan, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Iraq's Kurdistan region all coming under fire simultaneously is a marked departure from earlier retaliation rounds, which tended to concentrate on one or two targets at a time — this is Iran demonstrating breadth, not just intensity.

Precision, Claimed

Naming a Specific Hangar Sends Its Own Message

Publicly claiming a strike on a specific drone hangar, rather than describing a strike on "a base," suggests Iran wants credit for precision targeting — a deliberate rhetorical choice, regardless of what actually landed where.

The Physical-Evidence Problem

Mines Are Harder to Argue About Than Words

Unlike disputes over troop presence or route control, a tanker striking a mine is, in principle, a verifiable physical event — making this particular disagreement one where outside observers might eventually get real clarity, rather than permanent he-said-she-said.

Markets Keep Shrugging

A Four-Country Strike Barely Moves Equities

Stocks rallying on the same day this war expanded into four new countries is a genuinely striking data point about how thoroughly investors have priced in ongoing Middle East volatility as background noise rather than a fresh shock.

Escalation Math

Random Fact

Prince Hassan Airbase, the Jordanian facility the IRGC says it targeted Tuesday, is named for Jordan's former crown prince and has hosted U.S. and allied training missions for years — making it a long-familiar fixture of regional security cooperation rather than a newly established American presence.

Striking four separate countries simultaneously, rather than sequentially, is a deliberate demonstration of reach and coordination that a series of standalone attacks wouldn't convey nearly as clearly.
