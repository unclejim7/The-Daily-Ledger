Geopolitics · Markets · Wednesday Edition

Iran Fires Ballistic Missiles Toward the UAE as Trump Rules Out Talks and Iran Warns Gulf States Against Aiding Washington

UAE air defenses detected two ballistic missiles launched toward the country Tuesday, with one falling outside its territorial waters and the second landing within them; officials assessed the missiles as targeting maritime navigation rather than land targets. It's the first direct missile attack on the UAE in months, and a sharp escalation after weeks of the fighting concentrating on shipping and blockade enforcement rather than strikes toward Gulf states themselves. Iran's foreign ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei called the UAE's accusation "baseless."

The missiles landed the same day Trump flatly ruled out diplomacy, telling reporters "there are no talks or conversations going on, or scheduled, with the Islamic Republic of Iran" — a striking reversal in tone from Monday's Oman-Iran joint statement, which Trump's own team had been treating as a sign of progress just 24 hours earlier. Trump reiterated that the naval blockade remains "in full force and effect" while insisting, again, that the Hormuz Strait itself is "open and operating," a claim that continues to sit uneasily against ship-tracking data showing traffic still running at a small fraction of pre-war levels.

Iran, for its part, widened its own rhetorical target list Wednesday. General Ali Abdollahi, chief of staff of Iran's armed forces, warned that any Gulf state assisting the U.S. military would be considered a collaborator, writing on social media that "any assistance or facilitation provided to the aggressive US military will be regarded as participation alongside US military forces" and that the presence of American refueling aircraft at regional bases couldn't plausibly escape host governments' knowledge. The warning, following Trump's own threats against Oman this week, leaves several theoretically neutral Gulf states caught between two governments now explicitly threatening consequences for their cooperation, or lack of it, with the other side.

Markets read the moment as genuinely dangerous: Asian indices sold off sharply overnight, with South Korea's KOSPI shedding more than 5%, and U.S. futures followed lower. Tuesday's cash session had already closed down for a third straight day (S&P 500 −0.69% to 7,691.76, Nasdaq −1.33% on a chip-stock selloff, Dow −116 points to 53,343.40) as oil and yields weighed on sentiment even before the UAE missile news broke. Brent crude is now approaching $91.55 a barrel. Armstrong Economics reads Wednesday's Gulf-states warning as clear evidence the war continues widening rather than narrowing toward resolution — a direct threat against neutral or semi-neutral parties is characteristic of a conflict entering a more, not less, dangerous phase.

The Missiles
