Health, Wellness & Fitness

Research: Energy Drinks and Stimulant Supplements Linked to Sharply Higher Cardiac-Event Reports in Young Adults

Researchers at the University of Virginia School of Medicine, presenting at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions, reviewed more than 28,000 FDA adverse-event reports filed between 2017 and 2025. Products containing caffeine or other stimulants, including energy drinks and supplements, were named in reports involving cardiac arrest roughly eleven times more often, and heart attacks about four-and-a-half times more often, than other products in the dataset. Adults between 18 and 34 accounted for nearly half of the cardiovascular reports tied to these products. The researchers caution this is a preliminary, not-yet-peer-reviewed conference presentation based on voluntary adverse-event reports, which shows an association in reporting patterns rather than a measured individual risk multiplier, and the lead author specifically warned against interpreting the figures as a personal risk calculation.

The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

The FDA's adverse-event reporting system (FAERS for drugs, with a parallel system for other products) relies on voluntary submissions from consumers, doctors, and manufacturers, meaning its figures reflect reporting patterns rather than a complete count of every actual incident.

Word of the Day
disproportionality

(noun, from Latin dis- "apart" + proportio "proportion") — in safety research, a statistical measure of whether a product appears in adverse-event reports more often than its overall share of reports would predict. Today's energy-drink research is exactly this kind of disproportionality analysis.

Quote of the Day

“This shows an association in reporting patterns, not a personal risk calculation for any individual.”

— Paraphrased from the study's lead author, University of Virginia
