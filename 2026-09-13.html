Geopolitics · Diplomacy · Sunday Edition

Trump Rejects Saudi Request for Direct Strikes on the Houthis, Offering Intelligence Support Instead as Riyadh Weighs Acting Alone

President Trump has rejected a direct request from Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman to launch U.S. military strikes against Houthi forces following their seizure of Yemen's Red Sea coastline. MBS placed two phone calls to Trump on Thursday urging direct American action after Houthi forces completed their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait's Yemeni side. Rather than agreeing to strikes, Trump reportedly promised continued intelligence and targeting support — more than 100 U.S. military advisers are already in the kingdom assisting Saudi operations — while stopping short of direct American military involvement in a new front of the war.

Asked about the situation, Trump struck a notably casual tone: "everything's going to work out fine and dandy," he told reporters, a marked contrast to the gravity with which Riyadh appears to be treating the threat to its western oil-export routes. Saudi Arabia, for its part, has signaled a willingness to act unilaterally against the Houthis if the U.S. continues to hold back, and has already begun doing so in a limited way: Saudi aircraft struck Mocha's airport twice on Friday, targeting the facility now under Houthi control since its capture earlier in the week.

The diplomatic standoff over how to respond leaves the underlying energy-market problem unresolved. WTI crude officially topped $104 a barrel this weekend, its highest level since July 2022, while Brent pushed toward $110 as traders continue pricing in the combined disruption from both a substantially closed Strait of Hormuz and a newly Houthi-controlled Bab el-Mandeb. Whether Washington's preference for indirect support proves sufficient to protect Saudi Arabia's remaining export capacity, or whether Riyadh ultimately goes further on its own, may do more to determine oil's trajectory this month than any single data release.

U.S. equity markets are closed for the weekend, leaving Friday's rebound to stand (S&P 500 at 7,656.98, Dow at 52,573.29, Nasdaq at 26,333.04). Bitcoin eased to around $76,800 as the weekend's combined oil and war-scope news kept risk appetite in check, a reminder that crypto, unlike equities, doesn't get the benefit of a weekend pause from processing bad news.

A Deliberate Restraint

Washington Draws a Line at Direct Involvement

Offering intelligence and targeting support while declining to strike directly is a calculated middle path — enough to keep Riyadh from feeling abandoned, but a clear signal Washington doesn't want a third active front alongside Iran and the broader Hormuz campaign.

A Notable Tone Mismatch

"Fine and Dandy" Undersells the Stakes

A casual public dismissal of a crisis serious enough to prompt two same-day calls from a foreign head of state suggests either genuine confidence in the intelligence-support approach or a preference for downplaying a problem Washington would rather not escalate further.

Riyadh's Own Clock

Saudi Arabia May Not Wait Much Longer

Striking Mocha's airport twice in a single day, even before any formal decision to act unilaterally, suggests Saudi patience with the current support-only arrangement may already be thinner than public statements indicate.

The Number That Doesn't Wait for Diplomacy

$104 Oil Doesn't Care Who Strikes Next

Whatever Washington and Riyadh ultimately decide, WTI crossing its highest level since July 2022 is already a fully realized economic fact, not a contingent one — the market has priced in the disruption regardless of how the military response unfolds.

Geopolitics

A Familiar Shape

Support Without Commitment Has a Track Record

Providing intelligence and targeting assistance while avoiding direct strikes mirrors how Washington has approached several other regional conflicts in recent years — a posture that buys time but rarely resolves the underlying request for stronger action.

Reading Riyadh's Options

Acting Alone Carries Its Own Risks for Saudi Arabia

A unilateral Saudi campaign against the Houthis, without direct U.S. air power, would test capabilities Riyadh has previously struggled to deploy effectively against the same adversary in earlier phases of the broader Yemen conflict.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Reluctant Allies Are a Recurring Feature of Long Wars

Armstrong Economics has argued that as regional conflicts drag on, the original combatants' allies increasingly find themselves asked to absorb costs and risks they didn't sign up for — and that friction, more than any single battlefield event, often determines how far a war's supporting cast is willing to go. Saudi Arabia weighing unilateral action after being turned down by its most powerful backer reads on the site as exactly this kind of alliance-testing moment.

Random Fact

WTI and Brent crude, despite tracking closely most of the time, are actually different grades of oil from different regions — WTI from U.S. wells, Brent from North Sea fields — and their prices can diverge meaningfully when a disruption hits one region's supply chain harder than the other's, as this week's dual-chokepoint crisis illustrates.

Word of the Day
unilateral

(adjective, from the Latin unus, "one" + latus, "side") — undertaken by one party alone, without the agreement or participation of others. Saudi Arabia's floated unilateral option against the Houthis would mean acting without direct American military participation — a notably different, riskier posture than the coordinated approach Riyadh initially sought.
