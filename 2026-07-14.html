Geopolitics · Markets · Tuesday Edition

US and Iran Trade a Third Night of Strikes as CENTCOM Preps to Reimpose the Hormuz Blockade — Cooling CPI and a Bank Earnings Wall Collide on Wall Street

The ceasefire that Washington and Tehran signed last month is, for practical purposes, dead. U.S. Central Command carried out its third consecutive night of strikes against Iran overnight, hitting coastal defense systems, missile and drone sites, and maritime capabilities across Bushehr, Bandar Abbas, Mahshahr, and Abadan, and for the first time used one-way attack sea drones as part of the campaign. Iran responded by striking two UAE-flagged tankers, the Mombasa and Al Bahiyah, with cruise missiles inside Omani territorial waters, killing one Indian crew member and wounding eight others. CENTCOM says it will reinstate its naval blockade of Iranian ports at 4 p.m. ET today — the same posture that ran from mid-April to late May — and President Trump has floated charging a 20% toll on cargo transiting the Strait of Hormuz to cover what he calls the cost of "providing safety and security" to the waterway. Iran's self-declared Persian Gulf Strait Authority continues to insist the strait is closed to unauthorized routes; commercial crossings have fallen to roughly 39% of pre-crisis volume, with a rising share of tankers going dark — switching off their AIS transponders — to avoid detection on the trip through.

Oil is the clearest market signal of how seriously traders are taking this round. Brent crude settled Monday at $83.30, a 9.59% single-day gain and its biggest jump in more than six years, before climbing further overnight to top $86 a barrel Tuesday morning, the highest level in a month. WTI settled Monday near $78.14, up 9.4%, and traded close to $80 in early Tuesday dealing. Economist Mohamed El-Erian has warned a serious intensification could push crude toward the mid-$80s, though most desks still see oversupplied global inventories as a ceiling against a full repeat of earlier spikes. Equity futures fell sharply overnight on the renewed strikes before paring losses as attention rotated toward the day's economic calendar.

That calendar is unusually loaded. June's Consumer Price Index came in well below expectations Tuesday morning — up just 3.5% year-over-year, down sharply from May's 4.2%, with the seasonally adjusted monthly reading falling 0.4%, the largest one-month drop since April 2020 and far cooler than the 3.8% consensus. Ninety minutes later, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first semiannual Monetary Policy Report testimony to the House Financial Services Committee, a debut lawmakers had signaled would focus on price stability and the Fed's balance sheet. Layered on top: JPMorgan, Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo, and Citigroup all reported second-quarter results before the bell, kicking off earnings season with broad beats, while IBM's disappointing preliminary revenue sent its shares down more than 22%. Skyler Weinand of Regan Capital summed up the tension: the weaker inflation data likely keeps the Fed on hold for now, but "the best tool the Fed currently has is raising interest rates" remains the operative framework Warsh has signaled in every public remark since his confirmation.

Markets, given all of that to digest at once, settled into a cautiously positive tone. The S&P 500 advanced roughly 0.3% by mid-morning, recovering part of Monday's 0.79% decline, while the Nasdaq climbed about 1% as South Korea's memory giants rebounded from Monday's rout. The Dow lagged behind both. Whether that composure survives a full trading day that still has to absorb a resumed naval blockade at 4 p.m. ET is very much the open question heading into the afternoon.

Bank Earnings

JPMorgan Posts $16.9B Profit as Trading Desks Ride War Volatility — Goldman, Wells Fargo Beat, IBM Craters

JPMorgan earned $6.14 per share on $58.02 billion in revenue, both well above consensus, with equity markets revenue up 86% year-over-year as Iran-driven volatility again boosted trading desks; shares still slipped in premarket. Goldman Sachs posted $20.98 EPS against a $14.48 estimate and rose 4%. Wells Fargo's net income jumped 22% to $6.4 billion, with CEO Charlie Scharf crediting the bank's newly lifted asset cap, though shares fell nearly 2% regardless. Bank of America's profit rose 27% to $9.1 billion. IBM was the morning's outlier, plunging more than 22% after preliminary revenue of $17.2 billion missed the $17.86 billion Street estimate by a wide margin.

Chips Rebound

SK Hynix and Samsung Bounce Back a Day After a 9% KOSPI Plunge as Memory Stocks Lead Nasdaq Higher

South Korea's memory giants recovered part of Monday's steep selloff, helping lift Nasdaq 100 futures roughly 1% overnight and dragging U.S. peers Micron and Sandisk higher in sympathy. The bounce is fragile: Monday's rout was driven by a brokerage note questioning whether SK Hynix can hit its own quarterly profit targets, not by anything that has actually changed about underlying AI memory demand, so today's recovery says more about oversold conditions than a resolved debate.

Cooling Inflation

June CPI Falls 0.4% Month-Over-Month, the Sharpest Drop Since April 2020, as Annual Rate Slides to 3.5%

The Consumer Price Index rose just 3.5% year-over-year in June, down from May's three-year-high 4.2% and well below the 3.8% consensus, with the seasonally adjusted monthly figure falling 0.4% against an expected 0.1% decline. The reading reflects the retreat in oil prices that ran through most of June, before this weekend's renewed Hormuz escalation began pushing crude back up. Whether July's CPI captures that reversal is now the key question hanging over the Fed's July 28–29 meeting.

Naval Blockade Returns

CENTCOM to Reinstate the Hormuz Blockade at 4 P.M. ET — Trump Floats a 20% Transit Toll

Central Command says it will resume blockading maritime traffic entering and exiting Iranian ports this afternoon, the same posture that redirected more than 140 vessels and disabled nine non-compliant ships during an April-to-May run. President Trump has separately proposed a 20% fee on cargo moving through the strait, saying the U.S. should be "reimbursed" for providing security to what he called "a very rich portion of the world." More than 50,000 U.S. service members remain deployed across the Middle East, according to CENTCOM.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Warsh's Debut

Kevin Warsh Delivers His First Semiannual Testimony to Congress as Cooling CPI Meets a Fresh Oil Shock

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh testified before the House Financial Services Committee at 10 a.m. ET, ninety minutes after June CPI landed softer than expected. House Financial Services Chairman French Hill said ahead of the hearing that members would press Warsh on price stability, past policy missteps, and how he intends to manage the Fed's still-expanded balance sheet. The timing is awkward for anyone hoping for early clarity: today's cooler CPI print reflects June's retreating oil prices, but this weekend's renewed Hormuz strikes and the return of the naval blockade mean July's data could move the other direction entirely. Every public comment from Warsh since his confirmation has leaned hawkish, and Regan Capital's Skyler Weinand said the cooler print "likely keeps the Fed on hold for now" without changing that underlying posture. Senate Banking hears from Warsh Wednesday. CME FedWatch continues to price meaningful odds of a hold at the July 28–29 meeting, though those odds now hinge as much on where oil settles as on today's testimony.

Dark Shipping

IRS Update

IRS and Security Summit Partners Launch a Five-Week Identity-Theft Protection Campaign for Tax Professionals

The IRS and its Security Summit partners kicked off the summer "Protect Your Clients; Protect Yourself" campaign on July 7, a five-week series aimed at helping tax professionals guard client data against evolving tax-related identity theft schemes. The push follows the agency's July 8 rollout of Automatic Exemption from Penalty, which is phasing in this summer to replace First Time Abate for taxpayers with a clean three-year compliance history; some qualifying filers may still see penalty notices during the transition and can request First Time Abate in the interim. Separately, the IRS updated guidance this month on completing Form 2848, Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative, a routine but closely watched form for practitioners managing client authorizations.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Doomscrolling a War: What Constant Headline-Checking Does to Your Nervous System, and How to Stay Informed Without Staying Wired

Three straight nights of strikes, oil futures whipsawing, a Fed chair testifying while a naval blockade resumes at 4 p.m. — days like this one are practically engineered for compulsive news-checking, and the body pays a real cost for it. Repeated exposure to alarming headlines keeps cortisol elevated in a way that mimics chronic low-grade stress, even when nothing in a person's immediate environment has actually changed. Behavioral researchers who study this pattern generally recommend a few concrete boundaries rather than willpower alone: set two or three specific check-in windows rather than refreshing continuously, turn off breaking-news push notifications on days when the news itself won't meaningfully change your decisions, and notice the difference between staying informed and staying activated. Being current on a story unfolding across the Strait of Hormuz rarely requires minute-by-minute updates; it requires knowing the shape of it well enough to understand tomorrow's headline when it arrives.

Random Fact

The Fed chair's twice-yearly appearance before Congress traces back to the Humphrey-Hawkins Full Employment Act of 1978, which required the Fed to report to lawmakers on monetary policy every February and July. The law's actual reporting mandate technically expired in 2000, but the tradition survived on momentum alone — every Fed chair since has kept the twice-a-year testimony schedule voluntarily, meaning Kevin Warsh's appearance today is the 48th year running of a legal requirement that hasn't legally existed for a quarter-century.

Word of the Day
disinflation

(noun, economics, 20th century) — a slowdown in the rate of price increases, distinct from deflation, where prices are actually falling. Prices are still rising under disinflation, just more slowly than before. Today's June CPI is a textbook example: the 3.5% annual rate is still well above the Fed's 2% target, but the deceleration from May's 4.2% is exactly the kind of directional signal that can shift a central bank's posture, at least until the next data point complicates it.
