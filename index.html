Geopolitics · Iran · Tuesday Edition

Iran Targets the "US-UAE Oil Shuttle" Scheme, Offering a Possible Explanation for How the Strait Can Be Both Nearly Empty and Nearly Normal at Once

Iran's Supreme National Security Council secretary, Mohsen Rezaei, named a specific new target Monday: what he described as a "US-UAE oil shuttle" system moving Gulf crude out through the Strait of Hormuz region. According to Rezaei, small feeder vessels load oil at Arab ports and exit through a southern corridor near Oman's coast — largely avoiding the main shipping lanes Iran has policed most heavily — before transferring their cargo ship-to-ship onto very large crude carriers in the Gulf of Oman, with some feeder vessels then returning for additional loads. Rezaei claimed Iranian forces have been striking five or six participating vessels nightly for weeks, declaring flatly: "You are smuggling, but the strait is under Iran's control, and we manage it."

The shuttle scheme offers the first real explanation for a contradiction this edition has flagged for days: Secretary of State Rubio's claim that oil is flowing at nearly prewar levels and Sunday's IMF PortWatch figure of just four transits against an 85-a-day baseline can both be technically accurate if large-tanker transits through the strait's main lanes have collapsed while total regional oil volume is still reaching market through this alternate feeder-and-transfer route. Kpler data separately shows regional Gulf oil exports rebounded to 12.8 million barrels per day in September, the highest level since the war began — consistent with oil finding a way out even as traditional transit counts cratered. A separate IRGC claim that a supertanker was struck while transiting with its transponder switched off remains unconfirmed.

Markets closed mixed Monday: the Dow rose 0.38% to 51,850.55, the S&P 500 slipped 0.06% to 7,807.24, and the Nasdaq fell 0.44% to 27,245.90, weighed down by a nearly 4% drop in Micron Technology shares. Attention now turns to the start of earnings season and Wednesday's U.S. inflation report, which could reset the market's read on the Fed's next move.

Bitcoin traded choppily between $83,000 and $84,000, pressured by roughly $680 million in weekly spot ETF outflows and anxiety ahead of Wednesday's inflation data. The 30-year mortgage rate's last reading holds at 7.40%, with Freddie Mac's next weekly survey due Thursday.

Naming the Shuttle System Turns a Puzzle Into an Explanation

A New Front Opens Quietly

Targeting Feeder Vessels Is a More Granular Form of Pressure

Shifting attention from large tankers in the main channel to small feeder vessels in a workaround corridor suggests Iran is adapting its targeting to wherever oil is actually moving, rather than simply repeating pressure on routes that have already been largely shut down.

A Claim Still Unverified

"Five or Six Nightly" Is Rezaei's Number, Not an Independent One

As with so many figures in this conflict, the nightly strike count comes from the party with the clearest incentive to project strength, and deserves the same skepticism as any other unconfirmed claim from either side.

Legal Ambiguity at Sea

"Smuggling" Is Iran's Word, Not International Law's

Characterizing lawful ship-to-ship oil transfers in international waters as "smuggling" reframes ordinary maritime commerce as a violation requiring enforcement, a legal claim with no backing beyond Iran's own assertion of jurisdiction over the area.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Markets Always Find the Workaround Before Policy Catches Up

Armstrong Economics has long argued that commodity markets under blockade conditions reliably develop alternate logistics faster than either side's public statements acknowledge, since profit incentives move faster than military or diplomatic adaptation. The site's War Cycle model treats the shuttle scheme's emergence as exactly this kind of market adaptation outrunning the official narrative on both sides.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Restoring a Protein Called Menin Reversed Age-Related Changes in Older Mice Within 30 Days

Researchers found that declining levels of a protein called Menin are linked to aging, and that restoring Menin levels in older mice reversed several age-related physiological changes within just 30 days. Scientists say the rapid timeline and the breadth of changes observed make Menin a promising target for future research into interventions that could slow or partially reverse aspects of aging, though they caution that mouse results don't guarantee similar effects in humans.

Random Fact

A ship-to-ship oil transfer, the method Rezaei described as the backbone of the shuttle scheme, has long been a standard and entirely legal maritime practice used to load very large crude carriers that are too big to dock directly at many smaller ports.

Word of the Day
transshipment

(noun, from trans- "across" + shipment) — the transfer of cargo from one vessel to another en route to its final destination. The shuttle scheme Iran describes is, in essence, a transshipment operation reframed by Tehran as an illicit evasion of its control.
