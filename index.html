Geopolitics · Iran · Sunday Edition

Hormuz Attacks Hit Their Highest Weekly Total of the War as Traffic Collapses to a Fraction of Normal Levels

At least a dozen ships were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz and surrounding waters in the week ending October 5, the highest weekly total since the war began, according to Al Jazeera. The scale of the disruption is now measurable in stark terms: the IMF's PortWatch recorded just four transits through the strait on October 4, against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 a day, while Kpler data put average crude transits for the week ending October 7 at roughly 40% below normal levels. Two more outbound tankers were struck by unidentified projectiles on October 10 and 11, per UK Maritime Trade Operations, with one catching fire and the other damaged; two Indian seafarers remain missing after a drone strike on the tanker MT Petro Dolphin.

Notably, Saturday's reported naval mine strike — the one that led this edition a day ago — originates solely with Iranian state media and the IRGC and has not been independently corroborated, a caveat worth stating plainly given how quickly that claim shaped the weekend's coverage.

The scale of the broader campaign is becoming clearer from both sides. CENTCOM says that since the U.S. reimposed its naval blockade in mid-July, American forces have disabled four commercial vessels, turned around 135 ships, and destroyed ten tankers linked to Iran. On the diplomatic side, Iran is demanding a return to the now-collapsed memorandum of understanding reached in June, and while Tehran said on October 8 it would respond within days to Washington's reply on its seven-day Hormuz plan, no response has been reported yet.

Markets remain closed for the weekend; Brent crude settled at $104.72 on Friday, up 0.42% on the session. Bitcoin is holding near $83,000 after closing Saturday at $83,039, roughly stable following last week's sharp slide, and the 30-year mortgage rate's last reading stands at 7.40%.

A Number That Resolves the Ambiguity

4 Transits Versus an 85-a-Day Baseline Is About as Clear as This Gets

Weeks of competing claims about whether the strait is "closed," "restricted," or seeing "record flows" are hard to reconcile against each other, but an independent tracker putting daily transits at roughly 5% of the pre-crisis norm is about as close to an authoritative answer as this conflict has produced.

A Caveat Worth Stating Plainly

An Uncorroborated Claim Still Shaped a Full News Cycle

Saturday's mine-strike story traveled quickly specifically because it came from a source with an obvious interest in projecting strength, a reminder that speed of circulation and reliability of sourcing are two entirely separate things in this conflict's information environment.

The Blockade's Own Scorecard

135 Ships Turned Around Is a Number Washington Wants Known

CENTCOM publishing its own tally of disabled, turned-back, and destroyed vessels functions as a public accounting of the blockade's reach, giving outside observers a concrete benchmark against which to measure claims from either side going forward.

A Demand to Go Backward

Reviving a Collapsed Agreement Is a Notably Modest Ask

Iran demanding a return to June's failed memorandum, rather than pushing for new or more favorable terms, suggests Tehran may now see even a previously rejected framework as preferable to the status quo it's currently enduring.

Behind Every Tanker Strike Is a Crew

Tanker-strike coverage tends to focus on barrels and percentages, but the missing crew members from the MT Petro Dolphin are a reminder that each incident carries a human toll that the shipping and oil-market framing often leaves out entirely.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: A Demand to Revert to Failed Terms Often Signals Exhaustion, Not Leverage

Armstrong Economics has argued that when a party in a protracted conflict asks to return to terms it previously rejected, it typically reflects a recalculation that the earlier deal, however imperfect, now looks better than continued stalemate — a sign of diminishing options rather than renewed negotiating strength. The site's War Cycle model reads Iran's push to revive the June MOU as consistent with that kind of exhaustion-driven recalculation.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Scientists Discover a Previously Unknown Waste-Drainage Pathway Behind the Eye

Researchers identified a previously unrecognized drainage pathway at the back of the eye responsible for clearing cellular waste, a discovery made in mice that could open new avenues for treating conditions like glaucoma and macular degeneration. Scientists say understanding how this pathway functions, and what happens when it fails, may help explain why waste buildup contributes to vision loss in certain eye diseases and could eventually inform new treatment targets.
