Bitcoin · Markets · Saturday Edition

Bitcoin Gives Back Its Holiday Bounce, Slides 3.6% to Within $800 of Its Record Low

Bitcoin’s two-session bounce came undone overnight. The world’s largest cryptocurrency fell to roughly $59,090, a drop of about 3.6% from Friday’s ~$61,280 reading that erased its entire two-session bounce. That leaves it barely $800 above this publication’s all-time tracked low of ~$58,278 and some 28% below the ~$82,450 high set just two weeks ago.

The move landed on a weekend, when crypto is the only market open for business. Friday’s shortened Black Friday session left the S&P 500 at 7,653.07, the Dow at 52,300.39 and the Nasdaq at 25,901.30, and those closes stand until Monday. Brent crude held near $118 through a twenty-first straight quiet week in the Gulf, and the 30-year mortgage rate holds at 7.21% after Friday’s delayed Freddie Mac reading.

In Chicago, the Blackhawks edged the New York Rangers 2-1 at the United Center on Friday night, the Bulls are coming off a two-point win at Charlotte, and the 9-2 Bears enjoy a long post-Thanksgiving rest before hosting Jacksonville on Dec. 6. The weather cooperates with a mild Saturday near 50°F before rain moves in for Sunday.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Study: Leisure Exercise Linked to 24% Lower Dementia Risk — but Physically Demanding Work Tied to 20% Higher Risk

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in The Lancet Public Health, led by USC Dornsife’s David Raichlen, pooled 74 studies covering more than 4.2 million people aged 45 to 93 across 30 countries, with a median follow-up of about ten years. People with the highest levels of recreational activity — walking, cycling, running, swimming, sports — had a 24% lower risk of dementia than the least active, while those with the most physically demanding jobs had a 20% higher risk, an association that held after adjusting for age, education and chronic disease. The authors suggest the work-related link may reflect stress, low job control, unsafe conditions or pollution rather than movement itself, and caution that the underlying studies are observational and mostly rely on self-reported activity, so they cannot prove cause and effect. They stress the findings should not discourage anyone from physically demanding work — but that movement chosen for its own sake appears to be the kind that protects the brain.
