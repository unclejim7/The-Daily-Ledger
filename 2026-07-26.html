Geopolitics · Markets · Sunday Edition

Washington Goes Quiet on Iran for a Second Straight Night as Oman Brokers Progress on Reopening the Strait of Hormuz

For the first time since the current bombing campaign opened on July 6, the U.S. military did not report new strikes on Iran overnight Friday into Saturday—and by Sunday morning, that quiet had held for a second consecutive night. A U.S. Defense Department source told CNN operations are "on a hold," and Iran's army spokesman, Mohammad Akraminia, confirmed Tehran has likewise halted its retaliatory strikes on American allies across the Gulf. "These attacks continued until two nights ago, but over the past two nights the Americans have stopped their attacks," Akraminia said, adding that Iran's own retaliatory operations have paused in kind, even as the army warned the conflict would resume in full if U.S. strikes do. Two regional sources told CBS News that talks between Oman and Iran over reopening the Strait of Hormuz and adjoining territorial waters have made real progress, though both cautioned the diplomacy needs more time. Omani officials traveled to Tehran on Friday, and the visit coincided directly with the pause in U.S. bombing—officials said the strikes were intentionally held back so as not to disrupt the in-person talks.

Behind the lull sits a more contested debate inside the West Wing. Vice President JD Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine raised concerns about further escalating the war during a White House meeting with President Trump, according to people familiar with the discussion, as the administration weighs whether to press its advantage or seek an off-ramp. The pause also lines up with growing unease over munitions: U.S. officials have told CBS News the military cannot sustain the rate at which it has expended Patriot interceptors and precision-guided weapons since the campaign against Iran resumed, a strain that is complicating readiness planning for other potential fronts, including the Indo-Pacific. Iran's Health Ministry says the last month of strikes, from June 27 through July 24, killed 59 people and wounded 666; the broader 21-week war has produced more than 3,500 reported fatalities in Iran alone.

The lull has not stopped the war's edges from fraying elsewhere. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Saturday he will share evidence with Washington showing Russia has been feeding Iran satellite surveillance of U.S. bases in the Gulf, pointing specifically to Russian imagery of sites in Bahrain, Jordan, and Kuwait in the days before and after recent strikes there. That directly contradicts a Truth Social post Trump made Friday saying he takes Presidents Xi and Putin "at his word" that neither Russia nor China is arming Iran—a claim that sits awkwardly against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's own testimony to Congress this week that both countries are, at some level, "enabling" Iran's campaign. Meanwhile the Houthi-Saudi front that opened this week kept widening: the Iran-backed group struck two Aramco facilities in Jizan and Yanbu on Saturday in retaliation for Friday's Saudi airstrikes on Hodeida, and Pakistan's foreign minister worked the phones with his Saudi counterpart to try to contain the spillover.

Wall Street enters the week with its own pause to interpret. Friday's close left all three major indexes lower for the week even as the S&P 500 (+0.05% to 7,411.98) and Dow (+0.46% to 51,947.25) each ticked up on the day, with Apple's 3.5% jump doing the heavy lifting while the Nasdaq (−0.64% to 24,975.82) stayed under pressure from chip and megacap tech weakness. Brent crude's brief run above $100 a barrel Thursday gave way to a roughly 4% Friday retreat, and whether that holds through the weekend matters enormously for Wednesday's Fed decision: hike odds for July 29 sit near 38%, up from just 10.7% two weeks ago, almost entirely on the back of oil. A quiet weekend in Iran, if it lasts, is about the best news the Fed could ask for heading into its most consequential meeting of the summer.

Sentiment Watch

A Second Quiet Night Doesn’t Undo a Volatile Week — All Three Indexes Still Finish Lower

Friday's modest gains in the S&P and Dow couldn't erase a rough week for equities: all three major indexes closed lower for the second straight week even as Apple's earnings-driven pop and a late-day Reuters report on possible China-brokered U.S.-Iran talks helped stocks pare losses into the close. Bank of America's Bull & Bear sentiment indicator remains at its most bullish reading since 2021—a level that has historically preceded pullbacks—even as the macro backdrop stays genuinely unsettled heading into a live Fed decision.

Munitions Strain

Pentagon Officials Say Interceptor Stockpiles Are a Growing Constraint on the Campaign

U.S. military and intelligence officials tell CBS News the pace at which the Pentagon has expended Patriot interceptors and precision-guided munitions since strikes resumed in early July is unsustainable, and is fueling internal tension over how the Iran campaign trades off against readiness elsewhere, particularly the Indo-Pacific. That pressure is one plausible driver, alongside diplomacy, behind this weekend's pause.

Traffic Data

Hormuz Crossings Fell to a Trickle Thursday Even as Bab el-Mandeb Traffic Ticked Back Up

Intelligence firm Kpler recorded just six ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, down from 15 a day earlier, with most using Iran's own designated northern route rather than the U.S.-sanctioned southern one. Traffic through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait held closer to normal at 49 vessels, even with the new Houthi-Saudi blockade in effect, as some tankers rerouted flags to declare Chinese ownership to cross safely.

Gas Prices

National Average Hits $4.11 a Gallon, Up From $3.16 a Year Ago

AAA's national average for regular gasoline stood at $4.11 per gallon Friday, roughly a dollar above where prices sat this time last year, as the Iran-driven oil shock continues to work its way to the pump even after Friday's crude pullback. The White House has exempted select energy products from a new round of tariffs in an effort to manage the inflationary fallout.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Countdown to July 29

Hike Odds Hold Near 38% as the Weekend Oil Pullback Buys the Fed a Little Room

CME FedWatch continues to price roughly a one-in-three-to-38% chance of a rate hike at Wednesday's meeting, up sharply from just 10.7% on July 15, with essentially the entire move traceable to oil's spike toward $100 a barrel this week. Most economists surveyed still expect a fifth consecutive hold at 3.5–3.75%, and if Friday's retreat in Brent and WTI carries through the weekend and into next week, that would ease some of the pressure that built through Thursday. Fed Governor Lisa Cook has flagged inflation running at 3.7%, well above target, while Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and Governor Christopher Waller have both said policy may need reconsidering if price pressures don't cool. Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference Wednesday at 2:30 PM ET, delivered with deliberately reduced forward guidance, is expected to do most of the market-moving work regardless of the decision itself.

IRGC Says It Stopped Four Ships Trying to Run the Hormuz Blockade Even as Talks Advance

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said Saturday their forces stopped four vessels attempting to transit the Strait of Hormuz via what Tehran calls an "illegal and unsafe route," firing warning shots that forced the ships to alter course. The incident underscores that even as Oman-brokered talks on reopening the strait show progress, Iran continues enforcing its own routing rules on commercial traffic in the meantime.

Second Front

Houthis Strike Aramco Facilities in Jizan and Yanbu, Saudi Arabia Says

Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi rebels announced strikes Saturday on Saudi oil company Aramco's facilities in Jizan and Yanbu, firing what a Houthi spokesman described as dozens of ballistic and cruise missiles and drones, a day after Saudi Arabia struck the Houthi-controlled Red Sea city of Hodeida. Iran's foreign minister said Saturday there is "no military solution" to the Saudi-Houthi conflict and called for dialogue, while insisting the fighting "cannot be attributed to Iran."

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Vance Is Positioning Himself for 2028 — But No Candidate in This Fight Is Free of It

Armstrong Economics flagged Vice President JD Vance's escalating public break with the Israel-centric wing of U.S. foreign policy, noting the BBC's report that Vance has accused elements of the Israeli government of trying to manipulate American opinion against diplomacy with Iran. Vance's now-widely quoted line to Israel's critics of the MOU—"You're a country of nine million people. You can't just kill your way out of solving every single national security problem that you have"—is, Armstrong's write-up argues, an extraordinary statement from a sitting vice president, and one this weekend's White House meeting on de-escalation only reinforces. But the piece cautions against reading it as simple conviction: Vance's rise owes heavily to Peter Thiel's technology-and-surveillance network, even as Trump himself remains bound to donors who treat unconditional Israel support as a loyalty test—leaving Vance pulled between two competing power centers as he maneuvers toward 2028. The theme connects to Armstrong's broader argument from earlier in the week: unemployed Americans now outnumber job openings for the first time since April 2021, a structural signal Armstrong says fits the pattern where official statistics stop resembling reality well before the public catches on.

Real Estate & Mortgage Rates

Sunday Rate Check

Freddie Mac’s 30-Year Holds at a One-Year High of 6.58% for a Fourth Straight Weekly Increase

Freddie Mac's weekly Primary Mortgage Market Survey, released Thursday, put the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at 6.58% for the week ending July 23—its highest level since August 2025 and the fourth consecutive weekly rise, as Middle East tensions and inflation concerns continue pushing rates higher. The 15-year fixed rose to 5.96% from 5.93%. "As market conditions continue to evolve, borrowers should remember that shopping around for a mortgage rate can make a meaningful difference, potentially saving them thousands over the loan's lifetime," said Sam Khater, Freddie Mac's chief economist. A year ago the 30-year averaged 6.74%, meaning today's rate, while elevated on the year, still sits modestly below where it stood in July 2025. With this weekend's oil retreat and Wednesday's Fed decision both live variables, the path for rates into August remains genuinely unsettled.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Long-Term Study Links Coffee to a Healthier Liver — Lower Risk of Cirrhosis and Liver Cancer

A major long-term study published this week found that regular coffee drinkers had meaningfully lower risks of cirrhosis, liver cancer, and death from liver disease, along with healthier liver scans and blood protein patterns showing less fat, inflammation, and scarring than non-drinkers. The findings add to a growing body of research suggesting coffee's polyphenols and other compounds may activate protective mechanisms against chronic disease, echoing a separate American Heart Association review this month that found up to 400 milligrams of daily caffeine—roughly three to four cups—safe, and possibly protective, for most adult hearts. Worth remembering on a hot, humid Sunday: the same research doesn't extend to sugary iced versions loaded with syrup, and hydration still matters more than caffeine when the heat index is pushing 100°.

Random Fact

Oman, the mediator now shuttling between Washington and Tehran on the Strait of Hormuz, has played this role before: it was Omani back channels that helped produce the original 2015 Iran nuclear negotiations, a tradition that traces to Sultan Qaboos bin Said, who ruled for nearly five decades and cultivated Oman's reputation as the one Gulf state on speaking terms with virtually everyone—Iran, Saudi Arabia, the U.S., and Israel alike. That history is precisely why Muscat, not Riyadh or Abu Dhabi, keeps getting the call when the strait needs reopening.

Word of the Day
lull

(noun, from Middle English "lullen," to soothe or calm) — a temporary period of quiet or reduced intensity in the midst of ongoing activity or conflict, typically understood as provisional rather than final. The word carries an inherent tension: a lull invites relief while warning against complacency, since the underlying cause of the disturbance has not necessarily resolved. Two quiet nights over Iran, as CENTCOM and Tehran both describe it this weekend, is textbook lull—not ceasefire, not peace, just a held breath.
