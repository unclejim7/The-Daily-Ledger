Geopolitics · Markets · Friday Edition

Trump Threatens a “Massive Attack, Bigger Than Ever Before” as Iran Rejects a Ceasefire Offer and Oil Tops $100 — Tesla and Alphabet Selloff Sends Wall Street to Its Worst Day in a Month

The war entered its 13th consecutive night of U.S. strikes on Iran early Friday, with CENTCOM targeting sites around the capital of oil-rich Khuzestan Province, as President Trump told Axios Thursday he is weighing a “massive attack” on Iran that would be “bigger than ever before.” The threat followed word that Iranian and Iraqi officials had rejected a ceasefire proposal Trump delivered through Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi earlier in the week — Tehran's negotiating team declined the terms outright. Iran answered Friday morning with retaliatory strikes on U.S.-linked targets in Bahrain, Kuwait, and Jordan. Iran's Health Ministry now puts the campaign's toll at 55 killed and 629 wounded since the ceasefire collapsed on July 8.

The war's second front kept widening too. Yemen's Houthi rebels claimed fresh strikes on Saudi shipping Thursday, enforcing the naval blockade of Saudi ports they announced earlier in the week, and Reuters reports Iran flew IRGC commanders and missile-related equipment into Yemen this month to support the effort — ten to twenty-one senior personnel on a single July 13 flight from Tehran, according to two Iranian sources. Trump has said the U.S. will hold Iran directly responsible for the Houthi campaign. The State Department is weighing relaunching a 24/7 task force to assist Americans stranded in the region as the conflict's geographic footprint keeps expanding.

Oil is where the widening war is now landing hardest on markets. Brent crude briefly topped $100 a barrel Thursday for the first time since May before easing to around $97 Friday morning, while WTI traded near $85–92 across the session. Choice Institutional Equities estimates a full disruption across the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb Strait together could interrupt roughly 18 million barrels a day of crude exports. Kazakhstan has separately halted exports through the Caspian Pipeline Consortium terminal after drone attacks, adding another supply threat to an already-jumpy market.

Wall Street had its worst session in a month even before the overnight strikes: the S&P 500 fell 1.21% to 7,408.30, the Nasdaq dropped 2.15% to 25,137.69, and the Dow lost 506.93 points (−0.97%) to 51,711.65, with a gauge of megacap stocks suffering its worst session since April 2025's tariff rout. Alphabet fell 7% after lifting its 2026 capex forecast to $195–205 billion despite beating on revenue, and Tesla cratered 14.5% — one of its sharpest single-day declines ever — after an earnings miss, negative free cash flow, and cautious autonomous-driving guidance. Asian futures pointed lower again to start Friday as the oil shock and the tech selloff compound each other heading into next week's Fed decision.

Tesla

Tesla Sheds Over $140B in Market Value as Margins Compress and Free Cash Flow Turns Negative

Tesla closed at $319.69, down 14.5% — among its sharpest single-day declines ever — despite record Q2 revenue of $28.2 billion (up 26% year-over-year) on a Q2-record 480,126 deliveries. Operating margin fell to 1.4% and free cash flow turned negative for the quarter as spending on Optimus and robotaxi development accelerated. Trading volume ran 131% above the three-month average. Rivian and Lucid both fell in sympathy, down 4.2% and 4.9% respectively.

Alphabet

Alphabet Beats on Revenue and Cloud Growth, but a $205B Capex Ceiling Spooks Investors

Alphabet posted Q2 revenue of $119.8 billion, up 24% year-over-year and its 12th straight quarter of double-digit growth, with Google Cloud surging 82% to $24.8 billion. Nearly $100 billion in investment gains — including a stake in SpaceX now worth roughly $94 billion post-IPO — boosted net income. None of it mattered next to the raised 2026 capex forecast of $195–205 billion, up from $180–190 billion, which sent shares down 7% and dragged Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon lower in sympathy.

Oil Shock

Brent's Run Above $100 Reframes the Inflation Debate Just Days Before the Fed Meets

Brent's brief break above $100 a barrel Thursday — its highest since May — came as the Houthi blockade of Saudi ports compounds ongoing attacks in the Strait of Hormuz. A July 23 note from Choice Institutional Equities estimates a full disruption across both chokepoints could threaten roughly 18 million barrels a day of crude exports and 5 million barrels of refined products. Brent has since eased back toward $97, but the move has already reset the rate-hike conversation heading into next Wednesday's FOMC decision.

Earnings Wall

Intel Beats, SK Hynix's ADR Conversion Quota Fills as the Chip Sector Holds Up Better Than Big Tech

Intel shares rose in after-hours trading Thursday after Q2 results beat expectations, with early revenue from its first 18A external foundry customers cited as a bright spot. Separately, South Korea's securities depository confirmed the initial quota for converting SK Hynix local shares into Nasdaq-listed ADRs has been fully exhausted, with two-way conversion applications opening July 29. Western Digital jumped 12.5% on renewed Kioxia merger talk, a rare bright spot in an otherwise rough tech session.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Hike Odds Keep Climbing

July 29 Hike Odds Triple in a Week to Nearly 40% as the Oil Shock Complicates Warsh's First Real Test

CME FedWatch priced a mere 10.7% chance of a July 29 hike as recently as July 15; by Thursday that had climbed past 34%, and some trackers now show odds nearer 38–40% as Brent's run toward $100 forces the inflation debate back open. A 50-basis-point move remains effectively off the table — the live question is hold versus a quarter point to 3.75–4.00%. September odds have moved further still, with fed funds futures now pricing roughly 82% odds of at least one hike by then, up from just over half a week ago. Chair Kevin Warsh, who withheld his own dot from June's projections and called inflation “too high” at the ECB's Sintra forum, faces a genuinely live decision Wednesday: the statement and his 2:30 PM ET press conference — there is no fresh Summary of Economic Projections at this meeting — will do the work of repricing markets into September. Barclays' Ajay Rajadhyaksha argues the lack of demand destruction from elevated energy prices, layered on AI-driven price pressure, is pushing the committee toward a more hawkish stance than the market had priced even two weeks ago.

Yemen

Iran Sent IRGC Commanders Into Yemen This Month to Help Run the Houthi Campaign, Reuters Reports

Two Iranian sources told Reuters that between ten and twenty-one IRGC personnel, including senior commanders, flew from Tehran to Yemen on July 13 along with missile- and drone-related equipment, adding a direct Iranian hand to the Houthi blockade of Saudi ports and Red Sea tanker attacks that began this week. A Houthi media official who said he was aboard the flight denied Iran had sent military reinforcements. The report reinforces Secretary of State Rubio's framing that Tehran bears direct responsibility for the widening Red Sea front.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: A Record India-China Trade Number and Germany's AfD Both Point the Same Direction

Armstrong Economics flagged Thursday that India's imports from China hit a record $79.41 billion in the first half of 2026, up 21.8% year-over-year, even as Washington leans on New Delhi to decouple from Beijing — evidence, in Armstrong's framing, that capital and trade flows keep moving toward efficiency regardless of political pressure. A separate Germany post noted that the Alternative for Germany (AfD) has signaled that, should it enter government, it would pursue a peace push on Ukraine rather than continued Western-aligned escalation, part of a broader pattern of European political fragmentation Armstrong has tracked all year. Both items sit inside the same 2026 Directional Change thesis the Ledger has tracked since Edition No. 1: institutional consensus — on trade blocs, on war aims, on the shape of alliances — keeps eroding faster than official narratives admit, with the 2027 Panic Cycle escalation the next structural marker on Armstrong's clock.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

High-Impact Exercise Doesn't Damage Your Joints — Even If You Already Have Arthritis, New Research Suggests

A body of research highlighted this week by the Washington Post pushes back on a long-standing assumption that running, jumping, and other high-impact activity accelerates joint damage. Studies tracking people with existing hip or knee arthritis found no evidence that higher-impact exercise worsened their conditions, and in several cases functional outcomes improved compared with sedentary control groups. The practical takeaway from the researchers isn't that everyone should start sprinting tomorrow, but that fear of joint damage shouldn't be the reason someone avoids movement they'd otherwise enjoy — the deconditioning that comes from avoiding activity altogether tends to do more long-term harm than the impact itself.

(noun, from Latin conflagrare, "to burn up") — literally a large, destructive fire; by extension, a widespread and violent outbreak of conflict.
