Geopolitics · Markets · Sunday Edition

Iran Declares the MOU “Suspended” After an Eighth Night of U.S. Strikes as the American Death Toll Climbs to 16

The war entered its most dangerous phase in weeks Sunday morning as U.S. Central Command carried out an eighth consecutive night of strikes on Iran and Tehran responded by declaring the interim memorandum of understanding — the framework that had structured 60 days of on-again, off-again diplomacy since June — “suspended.” Explosions were reported in Bandar Abbas and on Qeshm Island early Sunday, and Iran said the U.S. had struck an under-construction nuclear power plant, while Kuwait reported that a power generation and water desalination plant had been hit for the second time in two days, with a resulting fire affecting a large number of electricity generation units. Bahrain and Kuwait both said Sunday morning that their air defenses intercepted fresh waves of Iranian aerial attacks, and Iran’s Revolutionary Guard claimed to have intercepted four ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz, a claim CENTCOM has not confirmed.

The immediate trigger for the weekend’s escalation was Friday’s deadliest incident for American forces since the war reignited two weeks ago. CENTCOM said two U.S. service members were killed and a third remains missing after Iran struck Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base with ballistic missiles and drones; four additional Americans were medically evacuated to hospitals in Jordan but have since been discharged. It was the first U.S. combat fatalities from Iranian fire since March, and it brings the total number of American service members killed in the conflict to 16, with more than 430 wounded. Iran said the strike targeted fuel storage facilities as part of the 14th phase of what it calls Operation Lightning. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth called the fallen troops “heroes” whose “sacrifice only stiffens our resolve,” while President Trump, asked about the deaths, called them “a very sad thing” and reiterated that Iran must not be allowed to prevail. The Pentagon said Saturday’s retaliatory strikes were ordered specifically to punish the IRGC forces responsible for the Jordan attack.

Wall Street already had its verdict in before any of this weekend’s news broke: Friday closed out the worst week for stocks in months, with the S&P 500 falling 1.01% to 7,457.69 and the Nasdaq dropping 1.40% to 25,520.24, capping a week in which the S&P lost roughly 1.6% and the Nasdaq fell 2.9% — a week that had far more to do with a Chinese AI model release and semiconductor capex jitters than with the war. Oil, however, was already pricing in the danger: Brent crude settled Friday at $88.10 a barrel, up 4.6% on the day and roughly 16% for the week, its third consecutive weekly gain, while WTI settled at $82.49, up 4.5%. Both benchmarks are now at their highest levels since mid-June. With the Jordan deaths, the fresh strikes on Iranian nuclear and Gulf infrastructure, and Tehran’s suspension of the MOU all landing after Friday’s close, Monday’s open looks set to test whether equities can keep shrugging off a war that markets spent most of July treating as background noise.

The suspension of the MOU is the more consequential diplomatic development. The framework, signed in mid-June after the Bürgenstock and Versailles rounds of talks, had never fully held — strikes and counterstrikes continued intermittently through its first month — but both sides had at least nominally remained committed to it as a vehicle for de-escalation. Iran calling it suspended, rather than simply violated, signals Tehran may now treat the 60-day negotiating track itself as void, raising the odds of a more open-ended campaign heading into August, a month Armstrong Economics’ cycle model has separately flagged as a potential inflection point for both Iran and Israel.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Heat Plus Wildfire Smoke Is a Compounding Risk — Not Just an Additive One

Public health researchers studying this summer’s repeated wildfire-smoke events note that heat and poor air quality don’t just stack on top of each other; they compound. High heat increases breathing rate and cardiovascular strain, which in turn increases how much smoke particulate a person actually inhales and absorbs into the bloodstream. Physicians point to older adults, children, pregnant people, and anyone with existing heart or lung conditions as facing the highest combined risk on days like today, when Chicagoland’s Air Quality Alert overlaps with building heat ahead of Monday’s front. The practical guidance holds regardless of which threat feels more pressing: stay in air-conditioned spaces rather than shade alone, keep windows closed, limit strenuous outdoor activity to early morning if at all, and don’t wait for symptoms like a scratchy throat or shortness of breath to escalate before moving indoors. A well-fitted N95 mask helps with the particulate matter but does nothing for heat stress, so the two risks require separate precautions, not one combined fix.

Random Fact

Kuwait, whose desalination plant has now been struck twice in two days, draws essentially all of its freshwater supply from desalination — one of only a handful of countries on Earth with almost zero reliance on rivers, lakes, or renewable groundwater. The country operates some of the largest multi-stage flash distillation plants in the world, a technology first commercialized in the Gulf in the 1950s precisely because the region has so little natural fresh water to begin with. That single point of dependency is exactly why the facility has become a repeat target.

Word of the Day
brinkmanship

(noun, Cold War-era coinage, mid-20th century) — the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the very edge of open conflict in an attempt to force a favorable outcome, without actually going over that edge. Coined to describe nuclear-era diplomacy, the term applies just as well to a 60-day MOU that both sides kept violating while insisting they still wanted it to hold — right up until one side declared it suspended.
