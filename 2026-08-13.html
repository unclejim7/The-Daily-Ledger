Geopolitics · Energy · Thursday Edition

Trump Says U.S. Might “Keep” Hormuz Permanently as Strait Traffic Collapses and Oil Stockpiles Hit a 40-Year Low

President Trump escalated his rhetoric on the Strait of Hormuz Wednesday, declaring the U.S. Navy blockade a "WALL OF STEEL" and asserting the United States has "total control" of the waterway — and might simply keep it. Trump argued Iran has no meaningful capacity to contest that control, saying the country "has no Navy, no Air Force," with unpaid soldiers and a "decimated" Revolutionary Guard. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi fired back Thursday, saying Trump "has long miscalculated due to intelligence failures" and suggesting Iran could "prolong" the war rather than accept terms dictated from Washington. War Secretary Pete Hegseth, for his part, said the U.S. military can sustain the blockade "indefinitely," citing enough ships to rotate crews through the region without interruption.

The rhetoric is escalating exactly as the physical reality of the strait deteriorates further. Traffic through Hormuz collapsed to just 3 vessel transits in the 24 hours ending Tuesday, down sharply from 14 the day before and a small fraction of the roughly 130 daily transits that moved through before the war. The consequences are now showing up in hard inventory numbers: U.S. crude oil stockpiles have fallen below 300 million barrels, their lowest level in more than four decades, and the International Energy Agency warns global oil inventories are set to fall by another 3.8 million barrels a day on average this quarter after a 4.2 million barrel-a-day drawdown last quarter. U.S. officials are now openly forecasting higher oil and gas prices for as long as the disruption persists.

All of this lands five days before the 60-day Oman-Iran memorandum of understanding expires Sunday, August 18. Araqchi has called the Oman piece "very close," but has repeatedly cautioned that a full reopening still depends on the U.S. honoring commitments from June's broader MoU — the same document both sides now accuse each other of violating. Oman itself has stayed deliberately ambiguous, declining to fully align with either Washington or Tehran's competing claims of control.

Markets, characteristically, told a completely different story Thursday: the S&P 500 cleared 7,800 for the first time, closing at a record 7,798.99 (+0.65%), with the Dow up 69.72 points to 53,839.99 and the Nasdaq gaining 0.81% to 26,803.03, all lifted by softer-than-expected wholesale inflation data easing pressure on the Fed. Oddly, oil fell more than 2% on the session despite the stockpile numbers — WTI to $81.25, Brent to $87.07 — a reminder that day-to-day trading and the underlying supply picture aren't always moving in sync. Freddie Mac's 30-year mortgage rate ticked down to 6.67%, its first weekly drop in six weeks.

The Real Number

Three Ships a Day Says More Than Any Speech

Whatever "total control" means rhetorically, three transits in 24 hours — down from 14 the day before, and a fraction of the pre-war baseline of roughly 130 — is the plainest possible evidence that the strait is not, in any functional sense, open.

The Inventory Problem

A 40-Year-Low Stockpile Number Changes the Stakes

U.S. crude inventories falling below 300 million barrels moves this from an abstract diplomatic standoff to a hard physical constraint on the American energy system, one that keeps compounding every day the strait stays effectively closed.

Talking Past Each Other

"Wall of Steel" Meets "Intelligence Failures"

Trump's escalating rhetoric and Araqchi's pointed rebuttal aren't negotiating positions so much as public messaging aimed at domestic audiences on both sides — which makes them a poor guide to what's actually happening in whatever back-channel talks remain before Sunday.

Records Anyway

7,800 for the First Time, Even as Oil Data Worsens

The S&P's first close above 7,800 arriving the same day as a 40-year-low stockpile reading and a strait traffic collapse is this week's cleanest illustration yet of how disconnected equity sentiment has become from the underlying energy story.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Belly Fat Plus Vitamin D Deficiency Linked to 123% Higher Death Risk

A study of more than 5,500 adults aged 50 and older found that people with both abdominal obesity and vitamin D deficiency had a 123% higher risk of death compared with those with neither condition, a striking combined effect well beyond what either factor alone predicted. Researchers said the findings suggest doctors may want to screen for both conditions together in older patients rather than treating them as separate, unrelated risk factors.

Random Fact

The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, established after the 1973-74 oil embargo specifically to cushion against supply shocks like this one, has itself been drawn down substantially over the past decade and now holds a fraction of its historical peak — meaning the 300-million-barrel commercial stockpile figure making headlines this week doesn't include a large emergency buffer the way it might have a generation ago.

Word of the Day
attrition

(noun, from Latin "atterere," to wear down) — a gradual, cumulative reduction in strength or effectiveness through sustained pressure rather than a single decisive event. Three ships a day, falling stockpiles, and a war of words that changes nothing on the water together describe a conflict now being fought, on the economic side at least, through attrition rather than any single dramatic turn.
