Markets · Federal Reserve · Tuesday Edition

10-Year Treasury Yield Tops 5% for the First Time Since 2023 as AI's Own Leaders Call for a Slowdown, One Day Before the Fed Decides

Two genuinely separate storylines converged Monday to produce one of the more consequential trading sessions of the year. The 10-year Treasury yield briefly surpassed 5% for the first time since 2023 and, by some measures, the first time since 2007, as mounting inflation anxiety collided with swelling government and corporate borrowing needs; the yield touched 5.01% before easing slightly as buyers stepped back in. Brent crude holding near $108 a barrel continued to feed the same inflation story that's driven markets for weeks, but Monday's yield move was historically significant in its own right — a level not touched in nearly two decades outside a brief 2023 spike.

At the same time, and unrelated to the war or the Fed, the artificial intelligence industry produced its own shock. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei argued in a Saturday essay that AI companies need to deliberately slow the pace of development on their most capable models given safety risks. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman responded by confirming OpenAI will not pursue its widely expected IPO this year, calling the timing "ill-timed" given the safety concerns Amodei's essay had crystallized — even as Anthropic itself continues planning a fall listing, reportedly on the Nasdaq, despite voicing the same underlying warnings. The dissonance between preaching caution and still proceeding to market drew notice on trading floors. Nvidia fell 3.4% and the PHLX semiconductor index sank nearly 6% as chip stocks bore the brunt of the selling.

The combined pressure pulled equities lower for the session: the S&P 500 fell 0.48% to 7,619.98, the Dow dropped 152.09 points (-0.29%) to 52,421.20, and the Nasdaq slid 0.56% to 26,186.41. Goldman Sachs revised its call for Wednesday's Fed decision from no change to an outright rate hike following Friday's inflation print, and market pricing now puts the odds of an increase near 90%. Bitcoin eased to around $76,500 and gold settled at $4,347.90, both continuing to trade more like rate-sensitive assets than inflation hedges as the countdown to Wednesday's decision enters its final stretch.

Whatever the Fed ultimately announces, Monday made clear that the coming decision is being shaped by forces well beyond the war alone — a bond market recalibrating around a level unseen in a generation, and an AI industry publicly wrestling with the pace of its own progress, both landing in the same 24-hour window.

A Level With History

5% Isn't Just a Round Number

The 10-year yield last spent meaningful time above 5% before the 2008 financial crisis, making Monday's brief crossing a genuine marker of how much borrowing conditions have tightened over the course of this war, not merely a symbolic milestone.

Saying One Thing, Doing Another

Anthropic's Own Contradiction Drew Notice

Warning publicly about the pace of AI development while proceeding with a well-timed IPO is the kind of tension markets tend to price skeptically — investors can read caution as either genuine principle or a savvy way to manage expectations ahead of a listing.

A Sharper Contrast

OpenAI's Delay Looks More Costly

Walking away from a widely anticipated IPO entirely, rather than proceeding despite safety concerns, is a materially larger signal than Anthropic's choice to continue — whether it reflects deeper caution or a genuinely worse set of internal findings isn't yet clear from the outside.

Two Shocks, One Session

Neither Story Needed the Other

The yield spike and the AI safety news arrived from completely unrelated sources, yet both pushed markets the same direction on the same day — a reminder that Monday's selloff reflects a session absorbing two real shocks rather than one narrative amplifying itself.

Random Fact

The 10-year Treasury yield is often called the "risk-free rate" in finance, serving as the benchmark against which nearly every other borrowing cost — mortgages, corporate bonds, even some student loans — is priced, meaning a move like Monday's ripples through the entire economy almost instantly, not just the bond market.
