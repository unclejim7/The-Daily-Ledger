Geopolitics · Markets · Tuesday Edition

Trump Threatens to Bomb Oman the Same Day Muscat and Tehran Publish a Formal Hormuz Statement

President Trump threatened Monday to attack a longtime U.S. strategic partner, telling Fox News in a phone interview that "if Oman gets in the way, we'll bomb the s*** out of them." It's the second time Trump has directed this kind of threat at Muscat this year; in May he told reporters "Oman will behave just like everybody else, or we will have to blow them up." Oman has served as one of the few consistent backchannels between Washington and Tehran throughout this war, making a threat against the mediator itself a genuinely unusual escalation, even by this conflict's standards.

The threat landed the same day Oman's foreign ministry published a formal joint statement with Iran on Hormuz navigation — the "understanding" both sides had been finalizing since Monday's route-map agreement. The two governments, describing themselves as the strait's Coastal States, reaffirmed commitment to safe passage under international law while emphasizing their own sovereignty over their territorial waters, and agreed to establish a joint working group between their foreign ministries to determine "the future administration of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz, the services that will be provided, and the costs associated with them, in accordance with international standards." They also agreed to consult with other littoral states and relevant parties — a detail notably silent on any formal U.S. role in that consultation.

Trump, for his part, showed no interest in softening his own position: he told Iran it should simply surrender, and claimed his administration has opened a direct backchannel with Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps. A senior Iranian official told Reuters separately that Tehran may abandon its defensive posture for an offensive one if diplomacy with Washington continues to fail — a notable hardening that arrived within hours of Trump's Oman threat.

Markets registered the tension directly. The 30-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.32% Tuesday, its highest level since 2007, as oil topped $85 a barrel with no sign of easing Middle East risk; Monday's cash session closed lower across the board (S&P 500 −0.52% to 7,745.06, Dow −272.63 points to 53,459.78, Nasdaq −0.32% to 26,644.91). Armstrong Economics argues that threatening a mediator, rather than an adversary, is itself a distinct category of escalation — one that risks removing the very channel both governments have relied on to keep talking at all, regardless of how the joint working group's eventual cost framework turns out.

A Different Kind of Target

Threatening the Mediator, Not Just the Adversary

Every prior threat in this war has been aimed at Iran directly; a second explicit threat against Oman targets the one country that's kept a functioning line of communication open between Washington and Tehran, which is a categorically different risk.

Real Progress, Narrow Scope

The Joint Statement Is Genuine, Just Limited

A formal statement on navigation, sovereignty, and a working group for future costs is real diplomatic output — but its notable silence on any formal U.S. role suggests Oman and Iran may be building a framework that functions with or without Washington's sign-off.

Hardening, Not Softening

Both Governments Escalate Their Rhetoric Together

A U.S. demand for surrender and an Iranian hint at offensive posture landing on the same day suggest neither side is currently negotiating toward compromise, whatever the Oman-Iran technical track produces on its own.

The Yield Story

5.32% Is a Number Worth Sitting With

A 30-year Treasury yield at its highest level since 2007 reflects more than a single day's Iran headlines — it's a signal that markets are pricing in sustained inflation and fiscal risk over the long run, not just this week's oil spike.

Trump's Oman Threat Isn't the First

The Fine Print

A Working Group, Not a Finished Deal

The joint statement's language on future "services" and "costs" for Hormuz navigation makes clear the toll question isn't actually resolved — it's been formally deferred to a bilateral Iran-Oman process, with U.S. buy-in to any eventual fee structure conspicuously unaddressed.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Attacking Your Own Channel Is a Structural Risk

Armstrong Economics flags Trump's repeated Oman threats as a distinct category of risk within the broader Panic Cycle framework: threatening an adversary can be posturing, but threatening the mediator itself risks collapsing the one functioning diplomatic channel this war has had for months. If Oman steps back from its facilitator role in response, Armstrong argues, that would represent a genuine structural setback independent of anything happening on the water in the strait itself.

Large Review Links Artificial Sweeteners to Higher Diabetes Risk, but Evidence Remains Weak

A systematic review and meta-analysis covering roughly 720,000 adults found an association between artificial sweetener consumption and higher diabetes risk, but researchers rated the certainty of evidence as low, noting only six studies were available and results varied substantially between them. The authors were careful to say the findings don't support automatically avoiding artificial sweeteners, underscoring how large sample sizes don't always translate into strong causal evidence when the underlying study designs are inconsistent.

The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

Oman has never fought a war with either the United States or Iran in the modern era, and has maintained diplomatic relations with both continuously since the 1970s — a rare balancing act in the region that is precisely why Muscat has functioned as a trusted go-between for decades of U.S.-Iran back-channel diplomacy, long before this war began.

Word of the Day
littoral

(adjective, from Latin "litoralis," of the shore) — relating to or situated on the shore of a sea or lake. Monday's joint statement's reference to consulting "littoral States" is a technical way of acknowledging that Hormuz's fate ultimately involves every country bordering the Gulf, not just the two negotiating the current route map.
