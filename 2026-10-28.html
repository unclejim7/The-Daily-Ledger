Federal Reserve · Markets · Wednesday Edition

Fed's Decision Looms as Aramco Confirms a Fire But Doesn't Address the Ras Tanura Refinery Claim Directly

The Federal Reserve's two-day policy meeting concludes today, with Chair Kevin Warsh's decision due at 2 p.m. ET and a press conference to follow. Markets continue to price a hold at 3.75%–4.00% at roughly 90% odds, with a modest tail probability on a hike and a December move still widely expected regardless of today's outcome. Warsh has offered no fresh signal since naming October and December his two "decision points" for the year, and his now-familiar refusal to preview outcomes has left traders with little beyond the futures market's own pricing to go on.

Aramco issued a statement overnight confirming a fire at its export terminal near Ras Tanura, describing it as "fully contained" with eight workers injured and operations otherwise unaffected — but notably did not directly confirm or deny Tuesday's Houthi claim of a ballistic-missile strike on the refinery itself. The Saudi-led coalition separately said it intercepted several ballistic missiles and drones over the Eastern Province and southern Saudi Arabia overnight. The result is a strange kind of clarity: something burned, is now out, and nobody official will say exactly what happened or why.

Markets shrugged off Tuesday's uncertainty well enough to post gains: the S&P 500 closed up roughly 0.6% to 7,722.60, within striking distance of its all-time high, while the Nasdaq jumped 1.2% to 26,477.81 on a chip-stock rally that spilled into crypto overnight. The Dow was essentially flat at 52,377.57. Brent crude held near $115.59 a barrel, still elevated after Tuesday's intraday spike past $118. Bitcoin rebounded to roughly $81,400, recovering from a weekend dip below $80,000 tied to the Middle East evacuation headlines.

In Tampa Bay, the Rays kept their season alive with a dramatic 12th-inning walk-off win Tuesday night, but Milwaukee still leads the World Series 3-1 and can clinch the franchise's first championship with a win tonight.

A Narrow Confirmation

Choosing to confirm only the fire's existence and containment — while leaving the cause and the refinery-strike claim itself unaddressed — is the kind of statement that satisfies a disclosure requirement without resolving the actual question everyone is asking.

The Saudi-led coalition's claimed interceptions of missiles and drones overnight confirm a genuinely active night in the skies over the kingdom, even as the specific question of whether the refinery was struck remains unanswered by any official source.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Selective Disclosure Is Itself a Data Point in the War Cycle

Armstrong Economics has long argued that what governments and state companies choose to confirm — and just as importantly, what they decline to address — carries as much signal as any official statement's actual content. The site's War Cycle model treats Aramco's narrowly worded confirmation as consistent with a combatant managing perception as carefully as it manages its own infrastructure.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Antibody Patterns May Predict Inflammatory Bowel Disease Years Before Diagnosis

Research presented at United European Gastroenterology Week, analyzing stored blood samples from 500 people collected up to a decade before diagnosis, found that elevated Epstein-Barr virus antibodies preceded Crohn's disease diagnoses, while rising antibody responses to herpesviruses and to the body's own proteins preceded ulcerative colitis diagnoses. Researchers caution the findings show association rather than proof of causation, but say the patterns could eventually support earlier screening for inflammatory bowel disease in people with elevated risk.

Random Fact

The Federal Reserve's modern practice of holding a press conference after every FOMC meeting only began in 2019; before that, the Fed Chair addressed the press just four times a year, leaving markets to parse the policy statement's language alone for months at a stretch.

Word of the Day
equivocate

(verb, from Latin aequus "equal" + vocare "to call") — to use unclear language to avoid committing oneself to a definite position. Aramco's statement overnight, confirming a fire while leaving the refinery-strike claim itself unaddressed, is a textbook act of institutional equivocation.
