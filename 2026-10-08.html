Geopolitics · Iran · Thursday Edition

Trump Says Iran's "Spunk" Is Fading as Oil "Surges" Through Hormuz, Even as Attacks Keep Climbing and Bitcoin Falls for a Fourth Day

President Trump said Thursday that Iran's "spunk" is decreasing even as oil exports surge out of the Strait of Hormuz, claiming more crude left the strait over the past three nights than at any point in its history, and predicting again that the war will end "very soon." He went further, calling Iran a "failed nation" buckling under the pressure of what the administration calls Operation Epic Fury, while separately warning of catastrophic consequences should Tehran strike American cities or obtain a nuclear weapon.

The claim of surging, record-setting oil flows sits alongside a now-familiar counter-reality: nearly 20 commercial ships have been attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz over the past month, and Brent crude has pushed back toward $105 a barrel on the continued threat to shipping. As in previous weeks, both things are apparently true at once — more oil is moving, and more ships are being hit — a pattern that's become one of this war's defining, unresolved contradictions.

Markets pulled back from this week's records Wednesday: the Dow fell 341.41 points (-0.66%) to 51,179.87, the S&P 500 slipped 0.22% to 7,801.77, and the Nasdaq eased 0.22% to 27,538.69, with the small-cap Russell 2000 the weakest major gauge, down 1.31%. Rising Treasury yields drove the retreat, as the 10-year climbed back near 5.31% and the 30-year near 5.68%, with investors digesting both the Fed's September meeting minutes and oil's renewed climb toward $100 a barrel.

Bitcoin extended its losing streak to a fourth straight day, falling to roughly $82,500 as Fed minutes signaling the possibility of further rate hikes combined with elevated yields to pressure crypto broadly; roughly $550 million in leveraged positions were liquidated, and spot Bitcoin ETFs saw a net $487 million outflow Wednesday, their largest weekly withdrawal yet. The 30-year mortgage rate's last confirmed reading remains 7.28%, with Freddie Mac's new weekly figure due later today.

A Familiar Pattern, Restated

"Record Flows" and "Record Attacks" Keep Being True in the Same Week

This is at least the second time in recent weeks that record-setting oil flows and escalating tanker attacks have been reported side by side, which should make both claims easier to evaluate on their specific merits rather than as evidence either side is simply winning.

A New Label for an Old Argument

"Failed Nation" Is a Rhetorical Upgrade From "Ready to Fold Up"

Each week's framing has grown slightly more absolute than the last, moving from predictions of capitulation to a blanket description of state failure — a progression that raises the bar for what would actually have to happen for the administration to declare victory achieved.

A Warning With No Specific Trigger

Catastrophic Consequences for a Scenario That Hasn't Happened

Warning of catastrophe over a hypothetical attack on American cities functions as deterrence regardless of whether Iran has the capability or intent to carry one out, which is likely the point — the warning's value lies in being stated, not in being tested.

Four Days Running

A Sustained Bitcoin Decline Is a Different Story Than a Single Bad Night

One volatile session can be dismissed as noise; four consecutive down days driven by a consistent set of concerns — yields, Fed policy, and war-linked oil prices — suggests a genuine shift in sentiment rather than a momentary shock working its way out of the system.

Escalating Language, Steady Facts

The Rhetoric Keeps Intensifying Faster Than the Underlying Situation Changes

"Fading spunk" and "failed nation" are qualitatively stronger claims than last week's "ready to fold up," even though the measurable facts on the ground — ongoing attacks, continued negotiations, a still-functioning if damaged economy — haven't shifted nearly as dramatically.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Escalating Rhetoric Without Escalating Facts Often Signals a War Entering Its Rhetorical, Not Military, Phase

Armstrong Economics has argued that when a conflict's public language intensifies faster than its actual battlefield or economic indicators, it often signals that domestic political messaging has become the primary front, with the war itself settling into something closer to a managed stalemate. The site's War Cycle model reads this week's rhetorical escalation against largely unchanged ground conditions as consistent with that pattern.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Starting MS Treatment Earlier Linked to Less Disability a Decade Later

A new analysis found that patients who began disease-modifying therapy for multiple sclerosis earlier after diagnosis showed significantly less disability ten years on than those who started treatment later, reinforcing a growing clinical consensus around early intervention. Researchers say the findings strengthen the case for prompt diagnosis and treatment initiation as a meaningful, long-term determinant of quality of life for MS patients.
