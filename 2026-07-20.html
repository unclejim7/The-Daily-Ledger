Geopolitics · Markets · Monday Edition

U.S. Death Toll Hits 18 as Ninth Night of Strikes Pounds Iran — Wall Street Shrugs It Off Again as Chips Rebound and Oil Pares a 4% Spike

The Pentagon disclosed Sunday that a third American service member died over the weekend, killed while disposing of a downed Iranian attack drone in northern Iraq on Saturday — a grimly technical death that lands alongside Friday’s combat losses in Jordan to push the total number of U.S. troops killed since the war began in February to 18. U.S. Central Command also confirmed Sunday it had recovered the remains of the soldier who went missing after Friday’s missile strike on Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base, closing out one of the war’s worst 72-hour stretches for American forces. U.S. officials also disclosed that Iran carried out three additional, previously undisclosed strikes on American positions in Jordan last week, wounding dozens of service members and damaging several helicopters — injuries the Pentagon did not publicize at the time, citing operational security.

The U.S. military began a ninth consecutive night of strikes on Iran Sunday evening, and a U.S. official told reporters Washington is surging additional airpower into the region — F-16s from Germany, F-35s from Britain — as an Israeli official said the U.S. is adjusting its regional force posture and preparing to receive more American refueling aircraft. The Washington Post reported a U.S. official saying flatly that “the U.S. is planning for a wider war,” while cautioning that dwindling stockpiles of air-defense interceptors and long-range munitions, along with strain on available troops and aircraft, will limit how far that expansion can go. “We do not have enough to safely sustain operations, and I don’t think the White House is aware of that,” the official said. Iran answered Sunday with strikes across the region: the IRGC said it targeted U.S. aircraft at Jordan’s Aqaba airport, hit U.S.-linked positions at Kuwait’s Al-Adiri camp and Ali Al Salem Air Base, and struck targets in Syria, while Kuwait’s government confirmed its desalination plant was attacked for a second straight day. The IRGC also claimed Sunday that two tankers “exploded and were forced to cease movement” while attempting to transit a Hormuz route Tehran has warned ships to avoid, though it stopped short of claiming responsibility for the strikes.

President Trump, asked about the mounting toll, framed it as evidence the campaign is working: “Iran has been very, very badly damaged. They’ve lost everything almost militarily,” he said, vowing the operation continues. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Sunday the administration remains open to diplomacy — “we’ve tried multiple times with Iran, and we’ll continue to try” — and Iran’s Foreign Ministry said it has received fresh proposals from international mediators, with spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei saying talks could resume if they serve Iran’s national interests. That crack of diplomatic daylight was enough to pull oil back from its overnight spike: Brent crude surged as high as $90.79 a barrel (+4%) and WTI topped $84.68 — both fresh highs since mid-June — before paring to roughly $87 and $82–83 respectively as the mediator headlines circulated. Energy Secretary Chris Wright insisted Sunday that Gulf oil exports remain resilient despite the chaos, running at “a little under 14 million barrels a day” — about two-thirds of pre-conflict volume — and pushed back on reports that shipping has largely halted.

Wall Street, for the second straight Monday, mostly looked past the war. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq opened higher and extended gains through the morning as chip stocks rebounded ahead of a marquee earnings week (Alphabet, Tesla, GM and AMD all report), while the Dow chopped between small gains and losses. CME’s FedWatch tool now prices an 87.7% chance the Fed holds rates steady at its July 28–29 meeting, even with gas prices back above a national average of $4 a gallon. In London, Andy Burnham was sworn in Monday as the UK’s new prime minister, replacing Keir Starmer — the country’s seventh PM in a decade — in a leadership change largely overshadowed by the deepening war six time zones away.

Oil

Brent’s Overnight Spike to $90.79 Pares as Iran Signals It Will Weigh Mediated Talks

Brent crude jumped as much as 4% overnight to $90.79 a barrel and WTI topped $84.68 — both the highest levels since mid-June — after the weekend’s strikes on Jordan, Kuwait, and the Strait of Hormuz. Both benchmarks pared roughly half of that spike by midday after Iran’s Foreign Ministry said it had received fresh proposals from international mediators and would consider resuming talks. The move still leaves crude up more than 15% over the past week, and AAA’s national average for regular gasoline has climbed back above $4 a gallon for the first time since the spring.

A War-Driven Oil Spike Complicates the Fed’s Case for a Hold Just as June’s Data Was Making It Easier

Kevin Warsh’s Fed heads into its July 28–29 meeting with markets still overwhelmingly expecting a hold — FedWatch has the odds at 87.7% — built on June’s cooler-than-expected CPI (3.5% year-over-year, down from May’s 4.2%) and a 0.3% monthly drop in PPI. Whether this proves as temporary as the ceasefire-era oil rallies that preceded it, or the start of a more durable repricing, is likely to be the single biggest swing factor heading into the blackout period before the meeting.

The framework Armstrong has held since Vol. I’s first edition continues: 2026 as the Directional Change, 2027 as the Panic Cycle escalation, 2028 as the potential systemic break.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

What Actually Keeps You Safe When Severe Storms Roll Through Overnight

With a slight risk of severe weather in the forecast tonight and the National Weather Service flagging “all hazards” as possible — damaging winds, hail, flash flooding, and isolated tornadoes — emergency physicians point to a short list of things that actually matter once storms are moving through after dark. The single most effective step is having a way to receive warnings that doesn’t rely on being awake and checking a phone: a NOAA weather radio or a phone set to override silent/do-not-disturb for emergency alerts. If a tornado warning is issued, the safest space is the lowest floor, most interior room, away from windows — a basement if available, or an interior bathroom or closet if not. Charge phones and flashlights before bed on nights with storm risk rather than during a power outage, and avoid using landline phones or plugged-in electronics during active lightning. For overnight severe weather specifically, the biggest documented risk factor isn’t the storm itself but sleeping through the warning, which is why a functioning alert system matters more than almost anything else on the list.

Random Fact

Disposing of an unexploded or downed drone — the task that killed a U.S. service member in northern Iraq this weekend — falls to explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) technicians, one of the U.S. military’s smallest and most specialized career fields. EOD techs undergo roughly 47 weeks of joint-service training before certification, among the longest initial training pipelines in the entire military, and the job carries a fatality rate that has historically run several times higher than the broader armed forces average — a risk that draws far less public attention than combat deaths from direct enemy fire, even though the danger is just as real.

Word of the Day
attrition

(noun, from Latin atterere, “to rub against”) — a gradual wearing down of strength or resources through sustained pressure, rather than a single decisive event. Originally a term for the erosion of rock by friction, it entered military usage to describe conflicts won not by a single battle but by slowly exhausting an opponent’s manpower and materiel. Eighteen deaths, four Jordan strikes, dwindling munitions stockpiles — the Iran war has increasingly taken on the shape of an attritional conflict rather than a short, decisive campaign.
