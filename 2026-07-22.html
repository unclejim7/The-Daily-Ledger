Geopolitics · Markets · Wednesday Edition

U.S. Strikes Iran for an 11th Straight Night as the Campaign Widens to Tehran’s Outskirts — Houthis Open a New Front With a Saudi Blockade

U.S. Central Command completed its 11th consecutive night of strikes on Iran late Tuesday, an approximately 75-minute operation that, for the first time in this phase of the war, reached the outskirts of Tehran and a military site near Tabriz in Iran’s northwest — a marked departure from the campaign’s earlier focus on southern Iran and the Strait of Hormuz corridor. CENTCOM said the targets included Iranian military operations centers, maritime capabilities, aircraft hangars, drone storage facilities, and logistics infrastructure, and that the goal remains degrading Iran’s ability to threaten commercial shipping; Washington says Iran has struck more than 30 commercial vessels transiting Hormuz over the past three months. Al Jazeera reported air defense systems activated in eastern Tehran and “massive explosions” heard in surrounding areas, though officials said there were no direct hits inside the capital itself. Iran's military said Wednesday it hit U.S.-linked sites in Kuwait, Jordan, and Bahrain in retaliation.

The war also gained a new front overnight: Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia, warning international shipping companies to reroute away from Red Sea approaches to the kingdom. A Houthi-run news agency said six vessels had already been forced to divert. With the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed to normal traffic for weeks, Saudi Arabia has leaned on an overland pipeline to move crude to Red Sea terminals for export — a workaround the new blockade now threatens directly, raising the stakes for a kingdom that had largely stayed out of the direct fighting.

Diplomacy is still technically alive but showing little traction. Iranian Interior Minister Eskandar Momeni met Tuesday with Pakistan's army chief and prime minister in Islamabad and Rawalpindi, seeking to revive talks through a key mediator, but no new framework emerged. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking at the ASEAN foreign ministers' meeting in the Philippines, said the U.S. remains open to negotiating but questioned whether Tehran is negotiating in good faith. President Trump struck a similarly dim note from the Oval Office Tuesday on the prospects for near-term talks, even as he has telegraphed that strikes would keep intensifying.

Markets, for a third straight session, largely traded around the war rather than reacting to it directly. Tuesday's close was strong — the S&P 500 gained 0.89% to 7,509.20, the Nasdaq Composite rose 1.29% to 25,837.21, and the Dow added roughly 384 points (+0.74%) to close near 52,223 — as chip stocks extended their rebound on strong Taiwan and South Korea export data. Wednesday futures slipped modestly as oil's overnight jump revived inflation worries, but the bigger story for markets is after today's close: Alphabet and Tesla report second-quarter results, the first two of the "Magnificent Seven" megacaps to weigh in on whether the AI capex story still justifies current valuations.

Oil & the War Premium

Crude Hits a 5–6 Week High as the 11th Night of Strikes and the Houthi Blockade Compound the Risk

WTI climbed roughly 3% to around $87 a barrel and Brent jumped near 4% to about $94, briefly topping $95 overnight, as traders priced in both the widening U.S.-Iran air campaign and the fresh Houthi threat to Saudi Arabia's Red Sea export route. The move extends a run that has taken Brent from the low $80s a week ago to its highest levels since the initial blockade scare in early June, with little on the diplomatic front to suggest near-term relief.

Housing

Existing Home Sales Unexpectedly Fell 2.4% in June, Missing Hopes for a Summer Rebound

Existing-home sales slipped 2.4% in June to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 4.09 million units, defying expectations for a seasonal pickup, according to data highlighted by Armstrong Economics. Federal Reserve & Inflation

Hike Odds Climb

Cleveland Fed's Hammack Adds to the Hawkish Chorus as Rising Oil Complicates the Path Into July 29

CME's FedWatch tool now puts the odds of a quarter-point hike at the July 28–29 meeting at roughly 24%, up sharply from just 18% two weeks ago, with the probability of at least one hike by September running near 69%. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack became the latest official to suggest further tightening may be needed, echoing Chair Kevin Warsh's own repeated line at the ECB's Sintra forum that "prices are too high." The Fed has held its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% all year, and June's dot plot already showed roughly half of officials penciling in at least one 2026 hike. The wrinkle heading into next week's decision is the same one that has shadowed the committee since the war reignited: crude oil's renewed climb toward $94 Brent threatens to reverse the disinflationary trend the Fed was counting on from June's cooler CPI and PPI readings.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Losing Just 80 Minutes of Sleep a Night Could Make You Gain Weight, New Research Finds

A study published this month found that sleeping roughly an hour and 20 minutes less each night for six weeks was enough to cause measurable weight gain and reduced physical activity in otherwise healthy adults — a shortfall well within the range many people experience during stressful stretches or busy news cycles like this one. Researchers say the effect isn't about willpower: less sleep disrupts hunger-regulating hormones and lowers the drive to move, creating a compounding cycle that's easy to fall into and hard to notice in the moment. The takeaway isn't perfection; it's protecting even a modest, consistent sleep window, since the study's participants weren't sleep-deprived in any extreme sense, just quietly shortchanged night after night. Small, sustained shortfalls add up in ways a single rough night doesn't.

Random Fact

The Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, the Red Sea chokepoint now central to the Houthis' new blockade threat, is barely 20 miles wide at its narrowest — named centuries ago by Arab sailors as the "Gate of Tears" for the treacherous currents and reefs that made the passage notoriously dangerous long before any modern conflict. Roughly 10% of global seaborne oil trade normally passes through it on the way to or from the Suez Canal, which is precisely why a blockade announcement here, like one in the Strait of Hormuz, can move markets before a single ship is actually stopped.

Word of the Day
attrition

(noun, military and economic strategy) — the gradual wearing down of an opponent through sustained, incremental pressure rather than a single decisive engagement. Rooted in the Latin atterere, "to rub against." Eleven consecutive nights of strikes, met by eleven nights of retaliation, with neither side achieving a knockout blow, is close to a textbook definition — a war measured in cumulative wear rather than any single battle's outcome.
