Geopolitics · Energy · Monday Edition

US and Iran Trade Fresh Strikes Overnight as Both Sides Dispute Whether the Strait of Hormuz Is Actually Open

The weekend's five-country barrage did not end the escalation cycle — it extended it into Monday. U.S. Central Command said American forces carried out a new round of strikes overnight, hitting dozens of additional targets including Iranian air-defense systems, coastal radar sites, and missile and drone capabilities, and for the first time using one-way attack sea drones as part of the campaign to degrade Tehran's ability to threaten Gulf shipping. Iran, for its part, continued to insist the Strait of Hormuz remains closed "until further notice," while Washington and CENTCOM maintained the opposite: that the waterway is open and vessels continue to transit it. Neither side's public position fully matches what's happening on the water.

That gap is visible in the shipping data itself. According to preliminary tracking analyzed by Bloomberg, a handful of commodity carriers have crossed the strait in recent days with their transponders switched off — so-called dark crossings that have outnumbered openly tracked passages for three straight days. Iran's self-declared Persian Gulf Strait Authority, which claims the right to control traffic through the waterway, warned Monday that any vessel using an "unauthorized route" would not be covered by its safe-passage guarantees, placing responsibility for "consequences" on the ship's owner, operator, and commander. Commercial transit through Hormuz has fallen to roughly 39% of pre-crisis volume, and just six vessels were tracked crossing during one recent 12-hour window versus 18 to 22 in a typical day earlier this month.

Oil markets moved first and hardest. Brent crude spiked as high as $79.29 a barrel overnight before paring back to trade near $78.14, up about 2.8% to 4% depending on the moment measured; WTI touched the mid-$74 range before settling closer to $73. Economist Mohamed El-Erian warned that a "significant intensification" of the conflict could push crude toward the mid-$80s, though most analysts still see a full repeat of last month's spike as unlikely given oversupplied global inventories and slow-recovering demand. U.S. equity futures fell sharply overnight — Polymarket's contract on an "up" S&P open Monday priced just a 22% probability — before the cash market opened in a split pattern: the Dow eked out a gain near 52,745, while the S&P slipped roughly 0.3% and the Nasdaq fell closer to 0.9% as chip stocks, still digesting SK Hynix's blockbuster Nasdaq Composite entry today, led the retreat. Overnight in Asia, South Korea's KOSPI plunged 9% and Japan's Nikkei fell nearly 2%.

The week's calendar does the market no favors. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first semiannual testimony to the House Financial Services Committee Tuesday morning, with June CPI landing just 90 minutes beforehand, followed by Senate Banking Wednesday. JPMorgan, Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo, and Citigroup all report second-quarter earnings Tuesday as well. Fed Vice Chair Michelle Bowman and Governor Christopher Waller both speak today, and ECB President Christine Lagarde meets with Warsh this evening — a lot of monetary-policy signal packed into 48 hours already crowded with unresolved war headlines.

Contested Waters

Iran's Self-Declared Strait Authority Warns Ships Off "Unauthorized Routes" as the Legal Fight Over Hormuz Continues

Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority, which asserts a right to direct traffic through Hormuz that most maritime law experts dispute, warned Monday that vessels transiting outside its preferred route forfeit any safe-passage guarantee. The UN's International Maritime Organization and shipping authorities have urged member states to reject that sovereignty claim outright, since the strait is governed by the principle of transit passage under international law — a principle CENTCOM has repeatedly cited in its own statements that commercial traffic remains free to move.

Tax & Accounting

IRS Update

IRS Rolls Out Automatic Penalty Relief, Replacing the Long-Standing First-Time Abate Program

The IRS announced last week that it is phasing in an Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP) this summer, a new process that applies failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalty relief automatically for taxpayers with a three-year history of timely compliance — no request required. AEP replaces First Time Abate for original returns with due dates on or after January 1, 2027, covering tax year 2025 and 2026 quarterly returns and beyond; some qualifying taxpayers may still see penalty notices during the transition period and can request First Time Abate in the interim. Estate and gift tax returns and other infrequent-event filings remain outside AEP's scope, and taxpayers who don't qualify can still pursue reasonable-cause relief. Separately, the IRS this week revised optional standard mileage rates for business, medical, and moving expenses effective July 1, 2026, citing recent fuel-price increases — a modification directly traceable to the same oil-driven inflation pressure dominating this week's macro story.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

The Season's Second Heat Wave Arrives Today — Simple Adjustments Prevent Most Heat Illness Before It Starts

With temperatures climbing to 90°F today and staying in the low 90s through much of the week, the biggest risk isn't any single hot afternoon but the cumulative effect of consecutive warm days and nights with limited recovery. The practical countermeasures are straightforward: move outdoor exercise to early morning before heat and humidity peak, increase water intake before feeling thirsty rather than after, and watch for early heat-exhaustion signs — heavy sweating, dizziness, and nausea — which respond well to moving indoors and rehydrating if caught early. Older adults, young children, and anyone on certain blood pressure or heart medications should be especially attentive, since those drugs can blunt the body's normal cooling response. Chicagoland's public cooling centers typically open when heat advisories are issued; checking in on neighbors without reliable air conditioning is one of the simplest and most effective things anyone can do during a multi-day stretch like this one.

The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

Every large commercial vessel is required to broadcast its position via AIS (Automatic Identification System) transponders, a safety technology born from the 1912 Titanic disaster's push for better ship-to-ship tracking. Switching one off is technically a violation of international maritime rules in most circumstances — which is exactly why "dark" crossings, like the ones tracked through the Strait of Hormuz this week, function as such a reliable signal of real operational risk: shipowners only accept that legal exposure when the alternative, being tracked by a hostile actor, feels more dangerous still.

Word of the Day
chokepoint

(noun, military and geopolitical usage, 20th century) — a narrow geographic passage, such as a strait or canal, whose control confers outsized strategic leverage because a small force can restrict or threaten traffic that has no practical alternate route. The Strait of Hormuz is the textbook example: just 21 miles wide at its narrowest point, it carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, which is why five months of intermittent conflict there have moved global energy prices more than almost any other single geographic factor this year.
