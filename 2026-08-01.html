Geopolitics · Markets · Saturday Edition

U.S. and Israel Prepare a Weekend Bombardment of Iran's Energy Infrastructure as Trump Weighs the Final Order

The United States and Israel are preparing what officials describe as one of the harshest bombing campaigns yet against Iran, this time targeting the country's energy infrastructure directly, according to CBS News. Strikes could come at any point over the weekend, but President Trump has not yet given the final "go" order as of Saturday morning. Notably, officials have discussed trying to conclude any campaign before financial markets open Monday, given concerns about the economic fallout of striking Iran's oil and energy sector directly—though no firm end point has been locked in, and the timing remains genuinely uncertain.

The reported plans arrive against an already tense backdrop. A tanker was struck by an unknown projectile near the Strait of Hormuz, with responsibility unclear as of Saturday, underscoring how precarious shipping through the corridor remains regardless of the broader campaign's next moves. Separately, Trump downplayed reports attributing a recent cyberattack on critical infrastructure in Minnesota to Iran, instead directing his criticism at the state's Democratic-led government—an odd domestic detour in a week otherwise dominated by the war's external stakes, but one that fits a pattern of the president resisting narratives that make the conflict's reach look larger than he wants to acknowledge.

Markets, closed for the weekend, at least got to end a genuinely wild July on a high note. Friday's session saw the S&P 500 climb roughly 0.7%, the Dow add about 276 points (+0.5%), and the Nasdaq rally around 1% after briefly giving back an early 1.3% jump—enough to hand the S&P its first winning week in three. The day's real story was underneath the index-level calm: Amazon shares surged nearly 15% by the close, its biggest single-day gain in more than a decade, on a blowout earnings beat, while Apple tumbled 7.2% as Services and China revenue both fell short of expectations despite otherwise solid results. The month as a whole was defined by that same tension—oil-driven whipsaws from the Iran war on one side, and mounting doubts about whether Big Tech's AI capital spending will ever show up in profits on the other.

If the reported energy-infrastructure strikes do materialize before Monday, oil markets reopening after the weekend will be the first real test of how much worse this phase of the war could get for the economy Trump has been trying to shield. Gold settled July near $4,076, and WTI crude sat around $84.61 late in the week—both levels that could move sharply depending on what, if anything, happens over the next 48 hours.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Healthy Vitamin D Levels in Middle Age Linked to Protection Against Alzheimer's Brain Changes

A study published in Neurology Open Access, drawing on 793 dementia-free participants in the long-running Framingham Heart Study, found that maintaining healthy vitamin D levels during middle age was associated with protection against brain changes linked to Alzheimer's disease. Researchers from the University of Galway say focusing on younger, cognitively healthy adults gives scientists a better window to study preventive factors before disease takes hold, rather than trying to reverse damage after the fact. In a separate but less encouraging finding this week, researchers linked high fructose intake to faster ovarian cancer progression following chemotherapy—a reminder that diet's effects on disease risk cut in both directions depending on what's being measured.

Word of the Day
infrastructure

(noun, from Latin "infra," below, plus "structure") — the underlying physical systems, such as power plants, refineries, pipelines, and grids, that a society or economy depends on to function, typically invisible until damaged. Striking a country's energy infrastructure specifically, rather than its military assets, is a qualitatively different kind of escalation: it targets the systems ordinary civilians and the broader economy depend on directly, rather than the forces prosecuting the war itself.
