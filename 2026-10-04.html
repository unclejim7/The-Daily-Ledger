Geopolitics · Iran · Sunday Edition

Six Tankers Struck in Hormuz in a Week as Iran's Central Bank Injects $2 Billion to Defend the Rial, With Washington and Tehran Still Talking Past Each Other

Two more tankers were struck by unidentified projectiles in and around the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, bringing the week's total to six. The first vessel, exiting the strait, suffered a small fire and blackout that crew extinguished before resuming its journey; the second, hit hours later east of Oman, reported no casualties and no environmental damage. Iran's central bank separately announced it would inject up to $2 billion to defend the rial, which kept sliding anyway as the currency's months-long collapse shows no sign of stabilizing even with direct intervention.

The strikes landed against a backdrop of negotiators still working from different scripts. Iran's Foreign Ministry reiterated that security in the Strait of Hormuz remains its "first priority" in talks and that reopening the waterway stays conditional on guarantees it hasn't yet received. Washington, meanwhile, continues to treat the nuclear file as its own top demand: the U.S. ambassador to the United Nations accused Iran on Friday of enriching uranium to 60 percent, a claim that keeps nonproliferation squarely at the center of the American position even as Tehran frames the entire conflict around shipping security. Qatari mediators are holding separate conversations with Foreign Minister Araghchi and with U.S. officials, but a deal requires each side to treat the other's top priority as something more than a secondary issue.

Trump repeated his now-familiar framing over the weekend: Iran is "ready to fold up," the war will end "very soon," and most likely "right after the election." The repetition itself has become part of the story, a deadline that keeps being restated without yet arriving.

Markets are closed for the weekend, so Friday's close stands: the Dow at 51,176.96, the S&P 500 at 7,722.72, and the Nasdaq near 27,302, an all-time high, with the 10-year Treasury yield having eased to roughly 5.23% off this week's 5.33%–5.34% multi-decade peak. Gold is holding in the $4,140–$4,160 range after Friday's decline, and Bitcoin is steady near $84,800 following another failed attempt to hold above $87,000. The 30-year mortgage rate remains at 7.28%, with the next official Freddie Mac reading due Thursday.

A Pattern, Not an Incident

Six Strikes in a Week Is No Longer an Isolated Event

Once tanker strikes accumulate to a weekly count rather than remaining isolated incidents, insurers, shippers, and governments all start pricing in a baseline level of risk rather than treating each strike as a shock — which is its own kind of grim normalization.

Money Can't Fix a Blockade

$2 Billion Doesn't Address the Rial's Actual Problem

Defending a currency with reserves works when the underlying pressure is speculative; it works far less well when the pressure comes from a literal naval blockade and a sanctions regime that's cut off the oil revenue the defense would normally be funded by.

Two Different Wars

Hormuz and Nuclear Enrichment Aren't the Same Negotiation

When each side's top priority is the other side's secondary issue, a deal doesn't require splitting the difference on one question — it requires each side to accept meaningful movement on the issue it cares about least, which is a structurally harder ask.

A Deadline on Repeat

"Very Soon" Keeps Getting Said Without Getting Closer

A prediction repeated often enough starts to function less as information about the war's trajectory and more as a recurring rhetorical device, one whose credibility depends entirely on whether it's eventually proven right.

When tanker strikes happen at a steady weekly cadence rather than in response to particular diplomatic setbacks, it suggests the attacks have become a feature of the war's current phase rather than a message tied to any one negotiating moment.

The Enrichment Claim's Timing

A Fresh Nuclear Accusation Right as Hormuz Dominates the Headlines

Raising the 60% enrichment figure now, while tanker strikes and currency collapse are driving the news cycle, keeps the nuclear file from fading from view even as the week's actual developments are almost entirely about shipping and sanctions.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Mismatched Priorities Are Often the Real Reason Wars Outlast Their Original Triggers

Armstrong Economics has argued that conflicts frequently persist not because either side wants continued fighting, but because each side's core demand sits lowest on the other's list of acceptable concessions — a structural mismatch that outlasts any individual round of mediation. The site's War Cycle model treats the Hormuz-versus-nuclear divide as exactly this kind of entrenched mismatch, one unlikely to resolve through mediation alone.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

REM and Deep Sleep Linked to Lower Risk of More Than 80 Diseases, Large Analysis Finds

A study analyzing sleep data from more than 95,000 people found that higher proportions of REM and deep (slow-wave) sleep were associated with a lower risk of more than 80 different diseases, spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological conditions. Researchers say the scale and breadth of the findings reinforce sleep architecture — not just total sleep duration — as a meaningful, modifiable factor in long-term health that deserves more attention in routine clinical care.

Word of the Day
intractable

(adjective, from Latin intractabilis, "not to be handled") — difficult to manage, control, or resolve. A conflict becomes intractable less often because of any single irreconcilable demand than because each side's priorities simply don't overlap.
