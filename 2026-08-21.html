Geopolitics · Markets · Friday Edition

Strait of Hormuz Traffic Collapses to Just Six Ships a Day as Bond-Market Relief Evaporates and Stocks Sink

Armstrong Economics reports that daily transits through the Strait of Hormuz have collapsed from a normal 130 to 140 vessels a day to just six — a number stark enough to move the story from military stalemate to full-blown supply shock. Armstrong frames the strait as no longer merely a chokepoint but "a tax imposed upon the entire world economy," one that shows up in freight costs, food prices, utility bills, interest rates, and government deficits well before it ever shows up in a ceasefire headline. The site also notes that the preliminary U.S.-Iran peace framework floated back in June has already collapsed in practice: Iran is demanding sanctions relief and compensation, while Trump has countered by demanding Iran itself pay compensation for deaths tied to the wars, attacks, and protests of the past several months.

Markets spent Thursday discovering just how brief Wednesday's bond-market relief actually was. The 30-year Treasury yield spiked back to 5.267%, erasing Wednesday's buyback-driven dip and pushing above where it stood before Treasury Secretary Bessent's intervention was even announced. The reversal reflects a deeper structural problem than any single day's policy move can fix: a widening federal budget deficit, inflation still running above target, and heavy corporate debt issuance competing directly with Treasurys for buyers. Bessent told CNBC Thursday that the buyback operation "could actually be larger" than the $4 billion already announced, but traders were unmoved. Equities followed the yield spike lower across the board: the S&P 500 fell 0.87% to 7,641.16, the Dow dropped 703.84 points (−1.32%) to 52,759.21, and the Nasdaq slid 1% to 26,067.17 — erasing more than the entirety of Wednesday's gains in a single session.

Bitcoin, characteristically, treated all of it as background noise. BTC topped $77,000 overnight, with spot Bitcoin ETFs pulling in $606 million in a single day, extending crypto's now-familiar pattern of decoupling from whatever the bond and equity markets are doing. Gold reacted the more conventional way, jumping more than 1% to reclaim the $4,580-an-ounce level for the first time since May, a genuine safe-haven bid as equities sold off. Oil, meanwhile, actually eased slightly — Brent to $93.38, WTI to $86.33 — even against the traffic-collapse backdrop, suggesting a meaningful share of the blockade premium may already be priced into crude after a month of Hormuz disruption.

The same yield spike that hit stocks flowed straight through to home-loan pricing: the 30-year mortgage ticked back up to roughly 6.70%, undoing much of Wednesday's brief improvement in a single day. Taken together, Thursday is a reminder that this war's economic and financial effects are no longer confined to oil tankers and equity headlines — they're arriving, however unevenly, in mortgage applications and retirement accounts as well.

The Number That Matters

Six Ships a Day Is Not a Blockade — It's a Shutdown

A drop from 130–140 vessels a day to six isn't a slowdown at the margins; it's the strait functioning, for practical purposes, as closed. That kind of number reframes months of incremental tanker-strike headlines into a single, much starker economic fact.

Framework Failure

June's Peace Deal Is Dead in Everything but Name

Neither side has formally walked away from the June framework, but the gap between Iran demanding sanctions relief and compensation, and Trump demanding Iran pay for war deaths, leaves essentially no overlapping ground left to negotiate from.

One Day, Erased

Treasury's Buyback Bought Barely 24 Hours of Calm

Wednesday's extraordinary intervention was framed as a genuine circuit-breaker for long yields; Thursday's full round-trip back above pre-intervention levels suggests the underlying deficit and inflation story is simply bigger than any single balance-sheet operation.

Divergence, Continued

Crypto and Gold Both Rally — for Opposite Reasons

Bitcoin's surge looks like risk-on momentum trading; gold's simultaneous jump looks like classic risk-off hedging. Both moving up together, on a day stocks and bonds both fell, is a reminder that "safe haven" no longer means just one thing to markets.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Two Playbooks, One Yield Curve

Treasury's Balance Sheet vs. the Fed's Rate Path

Wednesday's FOMC minutes showed three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a hike, not a cut; a day later, Treasury's own buyback push to lower long yields got overwhelmed by the same deficit and inflation concerns the dissenters were citing. The two institutions are pulling on the same lever from different directions, and Thursday's reversal suggests Treasury's grip on it is weaker than Wednesday's rally implied.

The Data Point

The Standoff

Iran wants to be paid for sanctions and damage; Washington wants Iran to pay for the human cost of the war. Two governments each insisting the other owes them money is not a promising starting point for any near-term de-escalation.

Armstrong Economics

Random Fact

Under normal conditions, the Strait of Hormuz carries roughly one-fifth of the world's total oil and gas shipments through a waterway just 21 miles wide at its narrowest point — making Thursday's report of just six daily transits, against a normal baseline of 130 to 140, one of the starkest single-number illustrations of this war's economic reach so far.

Word of the Day
chokepoint

(noun) — a narrow passage that constrains the flow of traffic, trade, or resources, and whose control confers outsized strategic leverage. Hormuz has always technically fit the definition; Thursday's six-vessel figure is the clearest evidence yet of just how completely that leverage is currently being exercised.
