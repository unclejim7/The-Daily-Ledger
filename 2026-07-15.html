Geopolitics · Federal Reserve · Markets · Wednesday Edition

Fifth Night of Strikes Collides With Warsh’s Senate Showdown — Cooling PPI and a $65K Bitcoin Breakout Lift Wall Street

The war and the Fed picked the same morning to demand attention. U.S. Central Command carried out strikes against Iran for a fifth consecutive day, part of a campaign now redirecting commercial vessels attempting to run the reinstated naval blockade — CENTCOM said Wednesday it had turned back two ships in the blockade's first 17 hours. Iran's government said U.S. strikes have killed more than 30 civilians in recent days and confirmed it retaliated Tuesday by targeting American military infrastructure in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan. President Trump, who floated a 20% "reimbursement fee" on ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz earlier this week, has since backed away from the toll in favor of trade deals with Gulf states. Iran's Foreign Ministry says it has no plans for negotiations and remains "focused on defending the country," while Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf accused Washington of "trying to weaken the Iranian arrangements through force."

In Washington, newly installed Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered the second day of his first semiannual Monetary Policy Report testimony, appearing before the Senate Banking Committee a day after his House debut. The session turned combative when Ranking Member Elizabeth Warren pressed Warsh repeatedly on whether he'd asked Fed Vice Chair Michelle Bowman about reports she attended a bankers' meeting during the FOMC's blackout period — the pre-meeting quiet period meant to keep markets from getting an information edge. "The tone that you are setting is one that seems to invite corruption," Warren told him. Warsh, confirmed by the Senate in the tightest vote for a Fed chair in U.S. history at 54–45, responded: "My commitment to you is to follow the law and follow the data. Follow our very best judgment." He otherwise held the line he's kept since his first press conference, largely declining to be drawn into fiscal or political questions.

The economic data gave both sides something to point to. June producer prices fell a seasonally adjusted 0.3% month-over-month — against expectations of no change — while the New York Fed's Empire State Manufacturing Survey jumped ten points to 15.6, its strongest reading in four years. CME FedWatch now prices an 85.6% probability the Fed holds rates steady this month. Markets took the mixed signals in stride: the S&P 500 traded around 7,562, up roughly a quarter of a percent by mid-afternoon, while the Nasdaq added close to half a percent. Chip stocks whipsawed after ASML raised its 2026 guidance for the second time this year, lifting the sector at the open before Micron reversed hard, falling 7-9% intraday alongside declines in AMD, Intel, and Lam Research — a rotation, not a rout, as investors moved into Big Tech names instead.

Bitcoin was the day's biggest mover by percentage. The token opened above $64,900 — its best level since June 17 — and touched a three-week high near $65,290 by mid-morning, up more than 4% on cooling inflation data even as the war entered its fifth day of strikes. Ethereum crossed $1,900 for the first time since early June. The divergence from prior weeks is notable: crypto had spent most of July shrugging off both good and bad headlines. Today it moved on the same disinflation narrative driving equities, a signal that Bitcoin's macro correlation may be reasserting itself heading into Thursday's Freddie Mac weekly mortgage survey and the 2 p.m. Fed Beige Book.

Morgan Stanley and BlackRock Beat as Second-Quarter Bank Season Rolls On

Morgan Stanley earned $3.46 per share on $21.35 billion in revenue, both well above the $2.94 and $19.64 billion Wall Street expected, with CEO Ted Pick crediting "active markets and consistent execution across all three regions." The results follow Tuesday's beats from JPMorgan, Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo, and Citigroup, rounding out one of the strongest bank-earnings openings in recent memory.

Chip Sector

ASML's Raised Guidance Lifts the AI Trade at the Open, But Micron Reverses Hard by Midday

Dutch equipment maker ASML raised its 2026 guidance for the second time this year, initially lifting AMD and Intel premarket. By late morning the move had reversed: Micron fell 7-9%, Lam Research dropped more than 4%, Intel and AMD gave back their gains, and SanDisk shed over 11%. The SMH semiconductor ETF slid roughly 2% even as Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Apple each rose 3-4%, suggesting a rotation into mega-cap tech rather than a broader AI-trade selloff.

Wholesale Inflation

June PPI Falls 0.3% as Gasoline Prices Drop 12% — A Second Straight Cool Inflation Print

The Producer Price Index for final demand fell a seasonally adjusted 0.3% in June, beating expectations for no change, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics attributing nearly two-thirds of the decline to a 12% drop in gasoline prices. Core PPI, excluding food and energy, rose just 0.2% against a 0.3% forecast. May's initially reported 1.1% increase was revised sharply down to 0.6%. Combined with Tuesday's soft CPI, it's the second consecutive day of inflation data undercutting the case for a near-term hike.

Regional Manufacturing

Empire State Index Jumps Ten Points to 15.6, a Four-Year High, on a Surge in New Shipments

The New York Fed's July Empire State Manufacturing Survey showed a substantial rebound in business activity, with the headline general conditions index climbing ten points to 15.6 — its strongest reading in four years — powered by a jump in shipments. New York Fed President John Williams, speaking separately Wednesday morning, said there are "encouraging reasons" to expect inflation's trajectory to keep improving.

Warsh's Senate Debut

Warren Accuses Warsh of Inviting Corruption Over a Blackout-Period Question He Wouldn't Answer

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's second day of semiannual testimony, before the Senate Banking Committee at 10 a.m., turned sharply adversarial when Ranking Member Elizabeth Warren pressed him repeatedly on whether he had asked Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman about reports she attended a meeting with bankers during last month's pre-FOMC blackout period — a stretch when officials are barred from discussing policy to avoid giving any investor an informational edge. "Did you ask?" Warren asked several times, without a direct answer. "The tone that you are setting is one that seems to invite corruption," she told him. Warsh responded that his commitment was to "follow the law and follow the data. Follow our very best judgment." Committee Chairman Tim Scott's opening focused on Warsh's independence, noting he was confirmed 54–45 in the tightest vote for a Fed chair in history — a fact Warren invoked directly, telling Warsh he "reportedly told the president exactly what he wanted to hear" in an earlier Oval Office interview, after which Trump said publicly that Warsh "thinks you have to lower interest rates." Warsh otherwise repeated Tuesday's House testimony nearly verbatim, opening with a tribute to the late Alan Greenspan and declining to be drawn into fiscal or political territory. CME FedWatch prices an 85.6% probability of a hold at the July 28–29 meeting, though traders note that probability moves with oil prices as much as with anything said on Capitol Hill.

Fifth Day of Strikes

CENTCOM Redirects Two Vessels as Blockade Enters Its 17th Hour — Iran Claims 30+ Civilian Deaths

U.S. Central Command struck coastal defense systems and cruise missile storage and launch sites on Greater Tunb Island Wednesday morning as part of a 90-minute wave intended to degrade Iran's remaining attack capacity, marking the fifth straight day of American strikes. "Since restarting the naval blockade against Iranian ports 17 hours ago, U.S. forces have redirected 2 commercial vessels attempting to run the blockade," CENTCOM posted. Iranian state media, citing Mehr and Tasnim, reported the strikes killed a navy lieutenant at the southern port of Jask; Iran separately says U.S. strikes have killed more than 30 civilians since the ceasefire's collapse. Retired Vice Adm. Robert Harward, a former CENTCOM deputy commander, said the U.S. has deliberately avoided the island's oil facilities because they are "a chunk of the world economy."

Trump Drops the Toll

Trump Abandons the 20% Hormuz “Reimbursement Fee” in Favor of Gulf State Trade Deals

President Trump has backed away from his proposal to charge a 20% toll on cargo transiting the Strait of Hormuz, telling reporters the plan would instead be replaced by trade deals with Gulf states. Iran's self-declared Persian Gulf Strait Authority continues to insist the waterway is closed to unauthorized routes; commercial crossings have fallen to roughly 39% of pre-crisis volume, with a rising share of tankers going dark to avoid detection. Trump told reporters this week the U.S. is "either going to make a deal or we're going to finish the job," adding, "I don't want to affect 91 million people."

IRS Update

Security Summit's Second Release Targets Phishing, Spear Phishing, Whaling & the “Security Six”

The IRS and its Security Summit partners' biweekly "Protect Your Clients; Protect Yourself" series moves into week two this week, shifting from last week's overview of emerging scams targeting tax professionals to a detailed look at phishing, spear phishing, whaling, and the "Security Six" — the baseline set of protections (antivirus software, firewall, multi-factor authentication, backup software, drive encryption, and a VPN) the agency recommends every tax practice run year-round, not just during filing season. Health, Wellness & Fitness

What Extreme Heat Does to the Brain, Not Just the Body — A Growing Body of Research on Heat and Cognition

With Chicago's Air Quality Alert running through midnight and heat index values well above 100°, most public-health guidance focuses on cardiovascular risk — heat stroke, heat exhaustion, dehydration. A growing line of 2026 research suggests the brain deserves equal attention. A meta-analysis pooling 34 heat-health studies found adults over 65 show roughly double the rate of cognitive decline as younger people during comparable heat exposure, and Maricopa County's health department linked 11% of heat-related emergency transports among adults 70-plus this summer to acute confusion rather than dehydration alone. The mechanism researchers point to is subtler than heat stroke: executive function — working memory, decision-making, impulse control — erodes well before body temperature reaches dangerous levels, particularly for renters without air conditioning or outdoor workers with prolonged exposure. The practical takeaway for a day like today: symptoms of heat-related illness aren't always physical. Sudden confusion, irritability, or disorientation in an older relative or neighbor warrants the same urgency as a fever or dizziness, and a phone check-in matters as much as a fan.
