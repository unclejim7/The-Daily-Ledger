Markets · Geopolitics · Weekend Edition

Bitcoin Posts Its Best Week Since 2024, Topping $78,000, as Iran Lays Out Its Price for Reopening Hormuz

Bitcoin capped its best week since 2024 on Friday, jumping 7.26% on the day alone to settle at $78,335 — a 22% gain for the week — as a weaker dollar and a friendlier regulatory tone out of Washington drove spot Bitcoin ETFs to their largest single-day inflow since May. The move extends a decoupling from equities and bonds that's defined crypto's performance all month, even as BTC remains well below both its 2026 high of roughly $94,820, set in mid-January, and its all-time high of $126,198 from last October.

Equities, for their part, closed out a rough week on a strong note. Friday's session — S&P 500 +0.4% to 7,674.37, Dow +1% (+517.80 points) to 53,277.01, Nasdaq +0.4% to 26,180.45 — rebounded from Thursday's yield-driven selloff, but all three indexes still finished the week lower after the round-trip in Treasury yields earlier in the week wiped out Wednesday's relief rally. Gold, meanwhile, extended its own run, trading above $4,500 an ounce and on course for a third straight weekly gain, as investors continue treating both bonds and stocks with some suspicion even as risk appetite returns to crypto.

On the geopolitical side, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, spelled out for the first time what Tehran would actually require to reopen the strait: Washington lifting its naval blockade and sanctions, withdrawing U.S. military forces from the region, paying war reparations, and releasing frozen Iranian assets. It's a demand list that runs almost exactly opposite the one Trump laid out earlier this week, when he said Iran should be the one paying compensation for the war's toll — leaving both governments now on record insisting the other owes money before anything reopens.

Armstrong Economics, in a fresh note over the weekend, points to a more practical arbiter of the standoff than either government's public position: "it is the shipowners, insurers, and commodity traders who determine whether a waterway is commercially open" — not Washington's insistence the strait is "open and operating," nor Tehran's insistence it remains closed. The site also flags a consequence showing up well outside the shipping industry: U.S. gas prices are now running nearly a dollar higher than a year ago, even as Hormuz traffic stays near its recent lows, exactly the kind of pocketbook impact Armstrong warned this week would eventually reach "freight costs, food prices, utility bills, interest rates, government deficits, and eventually elections."

A single week's 22% gain, capped by the largest daily spot-ETF inflow since May, is a different kind of move than the momentum-driven bounces crypto has posted earlier this month — this one looks like institutional allocators actually chasing the trade, not just short-term traders fading equity weakness.

A Rally With an Asterisk

Friday's Gains Don't Erase the Week

A 1% single-day pop in the Dow is a good headline, but all three major indexes still finished the week lower — a reminder that Thursday's yield-driven selloff did more damage than Friday's relief rally could fully repair.

Mirror-Image Demands

Both Governments Now Want to Be Paid First

Zolghadr's list and Trump's own compensation demand aren't just far apart on substance — they're structured as literal opposites, each side insisting the other must pay before anything else can move, which leaves essentially no starting point for negotiation.

The Conditions

Zolghadr's List Is the Most Specific Yet

Prior Iranian statements on reopening Hormuz have stayed relatively vague; naming four concrete conditions — blockade, sanctions, troop withdrawal, reparations, frozen assets — gives outside observers an actual checklist to measure any future negotiation against, however unlikely all four look right now.

At the Pump

The Blockade Has a Chicago Address Too

U.S. gas prices running nearly a dollar above year-ago levels is the clearest sign yet that this war's economic effects aren't confined to tanker headlines and equity screens — they're arriving directly at gas stations, including in Chicagoland.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Markets, Not Governments, Will Call It First

Armstrong Economics's weekend framing sharpens a point the site has been building toward all week: whatever Washington or Tehran say publicly about the strait's status, it's shipowners, insurers, and commodity traders who will actually determine, through their own routing and pricing decisions, when Hormuz is genuinely open again. The Panic Cycle framework's 2027 escalation forecast remains the site's central reference point, with this week's data points — the six-vessel figure, now this reopening-conditions list — treated as confirmation the trajectory hasn't changed.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

The Adult Brain May Repair Itself Far Better Than Scientists Once Believed

New research points to a specific group of support cells called astrocytes that respond actively to damaged brain tissue, suggesting the adult brain has meaningfully more capacity for self-repair than long assumed. Researchers say the finding could eventually reshape approaches to recovery from brain injury and certain neurodegenerative conditions, though they caution that translating the mechanism into actual therapies remains a much longer-term project.

Random Fact
