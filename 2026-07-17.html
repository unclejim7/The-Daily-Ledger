Geopolitics · Markets · Earnings · Friday Edition

Seventh Day of Strikes Hits Iranian Bridges and Rail as Tehran Strikes Al Udeid and Kuwait — Chip Selloff Deepens as Netflix Slides on a Weak Q3 Guide

The war entered its seventh consecutive day of American strikes overnight, with U.S. forces hitting bridges, a railway junction, and an airport across Iran's Hormozgan and Sistan-Baluchestan provinces in an operation aimed at cutting off the port city of Bandar Abbas from the rest of the country. Iranian state media reported the Geryveh and Kahurestan bridges in Khamir County were struck, severing a key route linking Bandar Abbas to Shiraz, while a railway junction west of Bandar Abbas and an airport further inland in Iranshahr were also hit. Iranian outlets put the death toll at seven to eight with roughly 20 wounded. Tehran's retaliation broadened the war's geographic footprint again: the IRGC said it struck Qatar's Al Udeid Air Base overnight, wounding four people — including a child hit by falling interception debris in a residential area — in the second direct hit on the base in eight days and the third Qatari target since March. Iran also struck power and desalination infrastructure in Kuwait and Bahrain, while a tanker was reported hit by a projectile off the coast of Oman and U.S. Marines boarded another vessel in the contested Strait of Hormuz. Strikes in Iraq's Kurdistan region, which Iran was blamed for, killed eight members of an exiled Iranian Kurdish opposition group near Sulaimaniyah. The attacks on Al Udeid land two days before U.S.-Iran negotiations are scheduled to resume in Oman, putting Qatar's mediating role under fresh strain.

Wall Street heads into Friday nursing a Thursday hangover that had nothing to do with the war. The S&P 500 fell 0.51% to 7,533.77, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.47% to 25,881.95, and the Dow shed 105.67 points to 52,552.97 as a second straight day of semiconductor selling overshadowed a strong batch of earnings. Taiwan Semiconductor posted a fifth consecutive quarter of record profit — revenue of $40.2 billion at the top of its guide, gross margin of 67.7%, net profit up 77% — and lifted its full-year revenue growth outlook to above 40% from above 30%. None of it mattered next to the number that actually moved the stock: TSMC raised 2026 capital spending guidance to $60–64 billion from $52–56 billion and tacked on another $100 billion in U.S. investment commitments, and shares fell more than 5% as investors priced in years of margin dilution from overseas fab construction. The selloff exported itself to Asia overnight, with SK Hynix down as much as 11% in Seoul and Samsung off more than 7%, extending Wednesday's Micron-driven rout across the memory complex. Netflix added its own gravity Thursday night, sliding more than 8% after hours on in-line Q2 results but a Q3 revenue guide of $12.86 billion against Street estimates near $13 billion — its third straight quarter of disappointing forward guidance even as the company posted a record $4.7 billion buyback.

Friday's premarket reflects all of it: S&P 500 futures were down roughly 0.8% and Nasdaq 100 futures off 1.6% to nearly 1.9%, putting major indexes on track for a weekly loss after three weeks of gains. Alphabet's Thursday slide of more than 4% on reports it is delaying its flagship Gemini 3.5 Pro model added to the AI-anxiety undertone, while a scattering of smaller premarket movers — AST SpaceMobile down 17% on a $1 billion convertible note pricing, Nebius down nearly 14% on New York's new moratorium on hyperscale data centers, Bloom Energy down more than 13% on short-seller reports — underscored how thin the market's patience has become for anything that looks like a financing or capacity red flag. The VIX, which closed Thursday at 16.73 (+6.76%), was indicated near 18.35 in early futures trading, up nearly 10%.

Oil is the one market where the war and the tape agree on direction. WTI traded near $80.64 and Brent near $85.76 Friday afternoon in Asia, both up roughly 2% as the widening target list — now including Gulf desalination and power infrastructure alongside the Hormuz shipping lanes — raises the odds of a second chokepoint opening at the Bab el-Mandeb Strait if Iran follows through on reported requests to the Houthis. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh wrapped two days of testimony this week without tipping his hand on the July 28–29 meeting, and a hotter oil tape gives the hawks on the committee one more data point to point to just as June's cooler CPI and PPI readings had started to build a case for a hold.

Co-CEO Greg Peters pushed back on engagement worries, noting members watched more than 97 billion hours of content in the first half of 2026, while the company repurchased a record $4.7 billion of stock in the quarter and narrowed full-year revenue guidance to $51.0–51.4 billion.

Chip Sector

TSMC's Record Quarter Can't Outrun Its Own Capex Guidance — Selloff Spreads to Seoul and Tokyo

Taiwan Semiconductor's fifth straight record-profit quarter — revenue at the top of its guide, gross margin of 67.7%, Q3 revenue guided to $44.6–45.8 billion — was overshadowed entirely by a roughly 15% increase to 2026 capex guidance and a fresh $100 billion U.S. investment pledge. Management said overseas fabs dilute gross margin by 2 to 4 percentage points during ramp-up, and traders described the reaction as a verdict on how high the bar has been set for AI-linked earnings rather than any demand concern. The selloff extended overnight to SK Hynix (-11% in Seoul) and Samsung (-7%+), a near-repeat of Wednesday's Micron-driven rout.

Big Tech

Alphabet's Gemini Delay and a Scattering of Financing Red Flags Widen Friday's AI-Anxiety Selloff

Alphabet fell more than 4% Thursday on reports it is pushing back the release of its flagship Gemini 3.5 Pro model, adding to a market already nervous about AI-capex payback timelines. A handful of smaller names amplified the mood in Friday's premarket: AST SpaceMobile fell 17% after pricing $1 billion in convertible notes, Nebius dropped nearly 14% on New York's new moratorium on hyperscale data-center construction, and Bloom Energy slid more than 13% on short-seller scrutiny of its scandium supply chain. None of the three moves relates directly to chips, but all three read the same way: investors are punishing anything that smells like a financing or capacity complication.

Global Rates

Bank of Korea's First Hike Since 2023 Underscores a Fed Facing the Opposite Problem

South Korea's central bank raised its policy rate a quarter point to 2.75% Thursday, its first increase since January 2023, after domestic inflation climbed to a post-2023 high of 3.2% in June, with officials flagging that large tech-sector bonuses risk spilling into broader wage pressure. The move is a reminder that even as U.S. inflation data has cooled over the past two CPI and PPI cycles, several major economies remain in tightening mode — a divergence that keeps global bond markets on edge just as oil's latest leg higher complicates the Fed's own read on where price pressure goes next.

Testimony Aftermath

Warsh's Careful Two Days Leave the July 28–29 Meeting as Genuinely Live — And Oil Just Made the Decision Harder

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh closed out back-to-back days of House and Senate testimony this week having largely avoided the kind of unscripted moment that moves markets, repeatedly telling lawmakers “my commitment to you is to follow the law and follow the data” while declining to signal which way a divided committee is leaning. That composure is being tested again Friday: WTI's roughly 2% jump on the widening Gulf conflict adds a fresh energy-price impulse just as June's cooler CPI (3.5% y/y) and softer PPI had started to build a case for holding rates steady. Markets still price meaningful odds of a hold at the July meeting, but the same dynamic that played out in May and June — a war-driven oil spike overriding an otherwise disinflationary data trend — is back in play with three weeks left before the decision. Bank of Korea's surprise hike Thursday adds a reminder that not every central bank is fighting the same fight.

Real Estate & Mortgage Rates

Freddie Mac Weekly Survey

30-Year Fixed Climbs to 6.55%, Its Highest Level in Nearly a Year, as Purchase Demand Softens

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey, released Thursday, showed the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaging 6.55%, up from 6.49% the week prior and the third consecutive weekly increase — the highest reading since August 2025. The 15-year fixed rose to 5.93% from 5.82%. Chief Economist Sam Khater said purchase application demand “has weakened recently, but housing affordability is more favorable and housing inventory continues to rise,” a backdrop he described as modestly improving for prospective buyers even as rates tick higher. The rise tracks a broader move in Treasury yields this week, with the 10-year settling near 4.557% Thursday. With no FOMC meeting until July 28–29, the next major catalyst for mortgage rates is likely to be how far this week's oil-driven yield pressure persists into next week's data.

Tax & Accounting

Fresh Treasury & IRS Guidance

Opportunity Zone Transition Rules and a Trump Account Gift-Tax Safe Harbor Round Out a Busy Guidance Week

Treasury and the IRS issued two notable pieces of guidance this week that practitioners should flag for clients. Notice 2026-40 provides transitional rules for Qualified Opportunity Zones and signals that proposed regulations governing the next phase of the program are coming, giving investors, developers, and fund managers an 18-to-24-month planning window before the framework tightens further. Separately, Rev. Proc. 2026-25 creates a safe harbor from gift-tax consequences for contributions to Trump Accounts, closing a drafting gap that could otherwise have required gift-tax reporting and eaten into a donor's available transfer-tax exemption. Both releases arrive as Treasury and the IRS unveiled their 2026 regulatory agenda on July 3, which emphasizes deregulation initiatives and the guidance still needed to implement the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Separately, the IRS's new Automatic Exemption from Penalty program — which began rolling out this month and covers 2025 annual returns and 2026 quarterly returns — continues to phase in; practitioners should still watch for penalty notices on otherwise-eligible returns during the transition rather than assuming relief has already applied.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

The Hidden Danger of a “Cooler” Heat Day: Why 87° and Rising Humidity Can Be Riskier Than It Sounds

After a run of mid-90s days, a forecast high near 87° can feel like relief — but public health researchers point out that heat-related illness risk doesn't track high temperature alone; it tracks heat index, which folds in humidity, and today's index of up to 95° sits squarely in the range where prolonged outdoor exertion carries real risk. Data compiled this summer shows heat-related emergency room visits running at extremely high rates in several regions, with the elderly, young children, outdoor workers, and anyone with cardiovascular or respiratory conditions most vulnerable. The practical guidance from health researchers is consistent: hydrate before you feel thirsty, take breaks in air conditioning rather than shade alone, and treat a “cooler” 87-degree day with the same caution as a 94-degree one if the humidity hasn't broken — today's scattered storms may finally deliver that break, but not until later in the afternoon.

Word of the Day
contagion

(noun, finance, from the medical/epidemiological term) — the spread of adverse market sentiment, selling pressure, or a crisis from one asset, sector, or country to others, often disproportionate to any change in the underlying fundamentals of what gets swept up in it. Friday's overnight action was textbook: TSMC posted its fifth straight record-profit quarter, and the resulting selloff still dragged SK Hynix and Samsung down double digits and low-single digits, respectively, a full ocean away.
