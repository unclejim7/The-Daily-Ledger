Markets · Iran · Monday Edition

Oil Surges and Bitcoin Tumbles as Iran Peace Talks Stall, Even as Trump Says Talks Will Resume This Week on a "Different Deal"

Markets opened the week sharply lower after last week's diplomatic momentum collapsed over the weekend. WTI crude jumped more than 4% to $96.44 a barrel as traders priced in growing pessimism that Hormuz peace talks are actually going anywhere, while S&P 500 and Nasdaq futures fell sharply Monday morning on the combined shock of surging oil and a fresh leg higher in Treasury yields, with the 10-year climbing to 5.23% — extending its highest levels since 2007. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi tried to keep the door open, calling Trump's rejection of Tehran's ceasefire proposal merely a "first reaction" and saying Iran still awaits an official U.S. response before abandoning the effort. Trump himself told Axios he expects negotiators to resume talks this week, though on a different deal than the one Iran originally proposed.

The disruption's real-world costs keep compounding: daily charter rates for very large crude carriers running the Persian Gulf to China have surged above $1.2 million as ships are forced onto longer voyages around the ongoing Hormuz chokepoint, shrinking the pool of vessels available for hire and adding fresh strain to global oil logistics regardless of how the diplomatic track resolves.

The week's tension also arrives alongside a sobering legal development: a UN-backed fact-finding mission has found "reasonable grounds" to believe the United States committed war crimes during the February 28 strikes that opened this war, citing two specific incidents — a Tomahawk strike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh Primary School in Minab that killed more than 150 people, including roughly 120 children, and a separate strike in Lamerd that dispersed tungsten pellets over a residential sports complex, killing 22 civilians. The mission's findings aren't a court judgment, but they could feed future accountability proceedings, and their continued reverberation adds real moral weight to a week already defined by diplomatic whiplash.

Bitcoin sank to around $83,000 and gold plunged 3.17% to roughly $4,149 Monday morning, both hit by the same combination of a strengthening dollar, rising yields, and fading hope for near-term de-escalation. The 30-year mortgage rate held at 7.03%, with Freddie Mac's next weekly reading due Thursday.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: War Crimes Findings Rarely Alter a Conflict's Trajectory, but Reshape Its Endgame

Armstrong Economics has argued that formal international legal findings, while they almost never change a war's immediate military or diplomatic course, tend to significantly shape the terms of whatever eventual settlement or accountability process follows once the fighting itself winds down. The site's War Cycle model suggests this week's UN finding matters less for today's ceasefire negotiations than for the shape of the postwar reckoning still likely years away.

Random Fact

UN fact-finding missions, distinct from the International Criminal Court, cannot themselves prosecute anyone — their formal role is strictly investigative, producing findings that other bodies, courts, or governments may later choose to act on, which is why today's report explicitly describes "reasonable grounds" rather than a legal verdict.

Word of the Day
indiscriminate

(adjective, from Latin discriminare, "to separate, distinguish") — lacking careful distinction or restraint, especially between combatants and civilians. It's the precise legal term at the center of today's UN war-crimes finding, and one of international law's starkest available descriptions of a military strike.
