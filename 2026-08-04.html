Geopolitics · Markets · Tuesday Edition

Hormuz Deal Could Land “Today or Tomorrow” as Wall Street Rides a Record Rally Into August

Genuine diplomatic momentum broke through Tuesday after days of claim-and-denial theater between Washington and Tehran. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping could come "today or tomorrow," restoring freedom of movement for vessels that have navigated the corridor under restriction for weeks. Qatar's Foreign Ministry, acting as a key mediator, described the broader U.S.-Iran negotiations as being "in very progressive stages," with drafts of a potential agreement already circulating among the parties involved. Separately, Iran's Foreign Ministry confirmed it is negotiating directly with Oman for a temporary safe shipping route through the strait, one that could last anywhere from one to three months while a fuller resolution is worked out.

None of this has come without friction. Trump, reacting to Tehran's latest denial that talks had resumed, called Iranian leadership "unbelievably duplicitous" over the weekend—a reminder that the same pattern of American optimism meeting Iranian denial that has defined this war's diplomatic track for weeks hasn't disappeared, even as the underlying substance (drafts being circulated, an Oman-brokered interim route under negotiation) suggests something real is happening beneath the rhetoric on both sides.

Markets, in any case, are treating this as genuinely good news layered on top of an already strong earnings season. The Dow closed at a record high Monday, surging nearly 700 points, and the rally continued into Tuesday with the Nasdaq up 0.8% at the open, the S&P 500 adding 0.5% and pacing for its own record close, and the Dow building on Monday's high with a further 1.5% gain. Caterpillar surged 12% after sales and revenue topped $20 billion for the first time in company history, and Palantir soared 18% on what CEO Alex Karp called an "otherworldly" quarter—both reinforcing the sense that this earnings season's strength is broad rather than confined to a handful of AI names.

Oil is where the Hormuz optimism is showing up most directly: WTI crude fell another 4.6% to $76.66, extending Monday's plunge and now sitting well below where it traded before last week's energy-infrastructure strike scare. With JOLTS data due Tuesday and the more consequential July jobs report landing Friday, this week's economic calendar will test whether the market's newly hawkish September rate-hike odds hold up against a backdrop of falling energy prices—normally a disinflationary force that would argue the opposite direction.

Regulatory Watch

Crypto Eyes the Clarity Act's August 10 Deadline

Bitcoin's modest recovery to around $63,776 comes as crypto investors focus increasingly on the CLARITY Act, the digital-asset market-structure bill that has cleared the House and the Senate Banking Committee but still lacks a floor vote. Senate Majority Leader John Thune has signaled the bill likely won't reach the floor before the chamber's recess begins around August 7, making August 10—the start of the Senate's state work period—the effective deadline for 2026 passage. Prediction markets have grown more skeptical: Polymarket now prices passage this year at 48%, down sharply from 74% a month ago, while Galaxy Digital's own institutional estimate sits closer to 60%. A miss would push the bill's fate to a mid-September reconvening at the earliest.

Random Fact

The Strait of Hormuz, at its narrowest point only about 21 miles wide, carries roughly a fifth of the world's total petroleum liquids consumption through shipping lanes just 2 miles wide in each direction—a chokepoint so consequential that a "temporary" one-to-three-month safe-passage arrangement, like the one Iran and Oman are reportedly negotiating, can move global oil prices more than most countries' entire annual production.

Word of the Day
duplicitous

(adjective, from Latin "duplex," twofold or double) — deceitful in a way that involves maintaining two contradictory positions at once, typically to deceive different audiences simultaneously. Trump's use of the word against Iranian leadership this weekend captures exactly the dynamic that's defined the diplomatic track of this war: claims of progress aimed at one audience, denials aimed at another, both issued by the same government within the same news cycle.
