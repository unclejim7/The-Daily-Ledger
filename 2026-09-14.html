Federal Reserve · Geopolitics · Monday Edition

Rate-Hike Odds Jump to 86.5% as a Fatal Hormuz Strike Forces Oman to Postpone Gulf Shipping Talks

Markets moved decisively over the weekend toward pricing in a Federal Reserve rate hike this Wednesday. According to the CME Group's FedWatch tool, the odds of an increase jumped to 86.5%, up sharply from 69.4% Friday morning — a shift driven by the combination of last week's hot CPI print and oil prices that are now up more than 11% over the past five trading days. Brent crude rose 2.75% to $107.48 a barrel and WTI gained 2.51% to $102.56, as traders continued digesting the dual disruption from a substantially closed Strait of Hormuz and a newly Houthi-controlled Bab el-Mandeb, where traffic has fallen to roughly 75% of pre-war levels even as some cargo reroutes around the chokepoint.

The diplomatic track took a fresh hit over the weekend as well. An Iranian cargo vessel was struck near Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday, killing one person and wounding four; Iranian state media blamed a "terrorist enemy" without naming a specific party, and the attack has not been independently attributed. The strike landed one day before Tehran was set to meet regional leaders in Oman specifically to discuss managing shipping traffic through the waterway — talks Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi confirmed Sunday were being postponed "in the interests of consensus," a diplomatic phrase that leaves unclear whether the delay reflects genuine disagreement or simply the optics of meeting so soon after a fatal attack.

Gold eased 0.22% to $4,340.58 as firming rate-hike bets pulled some of the metal's safe-haven appeal away, while Bitcoin held near $77,000 after a 4.4% weekly slide — both assets, along with silver and copper, moving in the opposite direction from oil as markets split ahead of Wednesday's decision. U.S. equity markets open for regular trading today, with Friday's close (S&P 500 at 7,656.98, Dow at 52,573.29, Nasdaq at 26,333.04) the last confirmed levels ahead of the bell.

Taken together, the weekend's news leaves the Fed walking into Wednesday with about as much inflationary pressure as it's faced at any point this cycle: a hot jobs report, a hot CPI print, and now oil at levels not seen since 2022, all arriving in the same two-week window. Whatever the committee decides, it will be doing so against the backdrop of a war that keeps finding new ways to complicate the numbers.

A Fast Move

Odds Jumping 17 Points in a Weekend Is Unusual

A move from 69.4% to 86.5% in roughly two trading days reflects how much new inflationary evidence — the CPI print, the oil spike, the widening war — accumulated over a very short window, rather than any single decisive data release.

Timing Speaks Loudly

A Strike the Day Before Shipping Talks Is Hard to Read as Coincidence

Whether or not the Qeshm attack was deliberately timed, its effect was the same either way: it gave Oman a ready justification to postpone talks that, if held, would have been the first sign of coordinated Gulf shipping management in months.

An Unusually Vague Accusation

"Terrorist Enemy" Names No One

Iran's own language avoiding a specific attribution is notable given how quickly Tehran has named the U.S. or Israel in other recent incidents — suggesting either genuine uncertainty about the attacker or a deliberate choice not to escalate rhetorically this time.

Rerouting Has Limits

75% of Normal Isn't the Same as Business as Usual

A quarter of Bab el-Mandeb's pre-war traffic still isn't moving through the strait even with rerouting underway, meaning the chokepoint crisis is producing real, ongoing losses rather than a fully absorbed disruption.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Compounding Shocks Are the Real Danger, Not Any Single Headline

Armstrong Economics has repeatedly argued that markets can absorb individual crises reasonably well but struggle badly when several genuine shocks — a hot inflation print, a real energy-price spike, a widening war, and a looming Fed decision — all converge in the same short window. The site's Panic Cycle framework treats exactly this kind of compounding, rather than any one data point, as the strongest signal of a coming volatility spike.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Very Hot Tea or Coffee Linked to Higher Esophageal Cancer Risk

The largest study of its kind, analyzing the hot-drink habits of nearly 980,000 people in the UK followed for more than 14 years, found that drinking very hot tea or coffee may significantly increase the risk of a type of esophageal cancer. Researchers say drink temperature itself appears to play an important role independent of caffeine content, suggesting a simple, low-cost behavioral change — letting hot drinks cool before consuming them — could meaningfully reduce risk for regular tea and coffee drinkers.

Random Fact

The CME Group's FedWatch tool, cited constantly during weeks like this one, doesn't survey economists directly — it derives implied rate-hike probabilities purely from the prices of federal funds futures contracts, meaning the "odds" reported are really just what traders are willing to bet, aggregated into a single number.

Word of the Day
attribution

(noun, from the Latin attribuere, "to assign, bestow") — the act of ascribing a cause or origin to something, especially an event or work. Iran's vague "terrorist enemy" language around the Qeshm strike is a striking case of withheld attribution — naming an outcome without naming a cause, a choice that itself carries meaning in a war defined by competing claims.
