Geopolitics · Markets · Sunday Edition

Trump Weighs Iran Energy Strikes Discussed at Camp David as Tehran Warns of “Full-Scale Regional War”

The plan to strike Iran's energy infrastructure directly, first reported Saturday, took clearer shape Sunday: the strategy was discussed during President Trump's cabinet meeting at Camp David on Friday, with Israel already notified and coordinating with U.S. forces on the potential operation, according to multiple sources. Power plants and refineries are understood to be among the likely targets, though Trump has not issued a final order as of Sunday morning, and Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell said the department would not discuss specific targets before the president decides. The by-now-familiar tension between wanting to conclude any campaign before Monday's market open and not having locked in an actual timeline remains unresolved.

Iran's response Sunday was its bluntest yet. Major General Ali Abdollahi, head of the country's wartime military command, said the United States "is moving at an accelerating pace to fan the flames of a full-scale regional war," a marked escalation in rhetoric even by this conflict's standards, while Iranian officials separately warned against the "flames of war" more broadly in comments to Al Jazeera. Whether that language reflects genuine alarm inside Tehran's command structure or calculated messaging aimed at deterrence, it lands at a moment when the war's own trajectory—widening rather than narrowing, per Armstrong Economics's running read on the year—gives it real weight.

Markets, closed for the weekend, at least have a clearer picture of what they're walking into Monday than the headlines alone suggest. Earnings season has been running unusually hot underneath all the war noise: of the roughly 300 S&P 500 companies that have reported so far, 85% have beaten estimates, with aggregate corporate profits tracking to grow more than 47%. RBC's Lori Calvasina reiterated her 8,150 S&P 500 price target over the weekend, arguing recent choppiness has actually opened up opportunities rather than closed them—though she flagged the approaching midterm elections, the interest-rate outlook, and August-September's historically weak seasonality as real headwinds. This week alone brings earnings from McDonald's, Kraft Heinz, Costco, Disney, Palantir, and AMD, any of which could get overshadowed entirely depending on what happens with Iran before Monday's opening bell.

Oil, the most direct read on how seriously markets are taking the energy-infrastructure threat, opened Sunday trading at $83.92 for WTI—essentially flat, suggesting traders are still waiting for confirmation rather than pricing in the worst case. Bitcoin ticked back up to around $63,455 overnight, a modest recovery from Saturday's two-week low. Whether either holds through Monday depends entirely on a decision that, as of this writing, still hasn't been made.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Is the Protein Boom Overselling What Most People Actually Need?

A sweeping review of more than 350 studies suggests popular high-protein diet trends may be overselling their benefits for most people, with evidence that moderately lower protein intake can improve metabolism, reduce inflammation and cellular damage, and activate pathways linked to longevity. The findings complicate the blanket "eat more protein" advice that's dominated fitness culture in recent years, suggesting the right amount depends heavily on individual activity levels and goals rather than a universal high-protein prescription. In smaller but related news, a separate four-month trial of 315 adults with central obesity found that just 75 minutes of brisk interval exercise once a week produced meaningful health improvements—a reminder that consistency, not extremity, tends to win in both directions.

The Daily Triptych

Random Fact

Iran's rank as the world's third-largest holder of proven natural gas reserves and ninth-largest for oil is precisely why "energy infrastructure" strikes carry such outsized economic weight compared with strikes on military sites: refineries and power plants take months or years to rebuild, while their disruption shows up in global oil markets within hours, which is exactly the asymmetry driving the reported effort to time any campaign around a market open rather than a battlefield calendar.

Word of the Day
asymmetric

(adjective, from Greek "a-" without, plus "symmetria," proportion) — lacking symmetry or balanced proportion between two sides; in conflict, describing a mismatch between a strike's cost to deliver and its cost to absorb. Energy-infrastructure strikes are asymmetric in exactly this sense: cheap and fast to execute, but slow and expensive to repair, which is part of why they function as economic pressure as much as military action.
