Geopolitics · Markets · Thursday Edition

Trump Says U.S. “Already Won Militarily” as Iran Buries Khamenei in Mashhad — Markets Shrug Off a Second Night of Strikes

Iran lays its slain Supreme Leader to rest Thursday at the Imam Reza shrine in Mashhad, the culmination of a six-day state funeral that began July 4 and has drawn what Iranian officials describe as tens of millions of mourners across Tehran, Qom, Najaf, and Karbala. The burial ceremony, delayed to roughly 2:30 PM local time by crowds larger than organizers anticipated, unfolds even as the military exchange that flared Tuesday shows no sign of cooling: the U.S. struck more than 80 Iranian targets overnight for a second consecutive night, and Iran answered with further projectiles toward U.S.-linked sites in the Gulf. Speaking in Ankara at the NATO summit, President Trump struck a notably different tone than Wednesday's "it's over" declaration, telling reporters "we have many ways we can win, but we've already won militarily" while suggesting Iran has "very little left" and still wants a deal. Iran's negotiators, led by parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, continue to insist Washington broke the June memorandum first by reimposing oil sanctions.

Mojtaba Khamenei, proclaimed successor days after his father's assassination in February, has still not appeared in public at any point during the funeral; Iranian sources say he remains too injured from the strike that killed his father to manage a public appearance, and security services are limiting his exposure given the ongoing strikes. Overnight U.S. attacks reportedly knocked out the Tehran-Mashhad rail line, though Iranian state television said the disruption would not delay Thursday's burial. The scale of the event — schools, mosques, and sports halls converted to lodging, highways turned into temporary parking, flights disrupted across multiple cities — makes it one of the largest funerals in modern history, with organizers working to avoid a repeat of the fatal 2020 crowd crush at Qasem Soleimani's funeral.

Wall Street, for its part, largely looked past the overnight escalation. Wednesday's session was rough — the Dow fell 576.76 points (-1.09%) to 52,348.39, its sharpest drop since the ceasefire first came under strain, while the S&P 500 slipped 0.28% to 7,482.71 and the Nasdaq eked out a 0.20% gain as chipmakers held up. Thursday morning told a different story: Nasdaq 100 futures climbed as much as 0.9% and S&P futures added roughly 0.1-0.3% as traders refocused on the AI trade ahead of SK Hynix's roughly $28 billion Nasdaq debut, with Dow futures little changed. Oil, which spiked more than 5% Wednesday to its highest levels since the ceasefire began, gave back some of that move Thursday morning as markets tried to gauge whether the latest flare-up represents a genuine reopening of the war or another round of the tit-for-tat pattern that has repeated for weeks.

Layered on top of the geopolitical whiplash: minutes from the Fed's June 16-17 meeting, released Wednesday afternoon, showed a committee split exactly down the middle on a 2026 rate hike — nine of 18 officials penciled one in, nine did not. Chair Kevin Warsh withheld his own projection from the dot plot for the first time in the tool's history, leaving Wednesday's transcript as the clearest on-record window into his thinking before the July 28-29 meeting. With oil back on the move and the Iran conflict reopened, the minutes' hawkish undertone — "the minutes demonstrated the Fed's hawkish bias," in the words of one wealth-management strategist — suddenly reads very differently than it would have 48 hours ago.

FOMC Minutes

June Minutes Show Committee Split 9-9 on a 2026 Hike — Warsh Withheld His Own Dot

Minutes from the Fed's June 16-17 meeting, released Wednesday at 2 PM ET, showed nine of 18 officials penciling in at least one 2026 rate hike against nine who did not — an exact split. Chair Kevin Warsh withheld his own dot-plot projection, the first chair ever to do so, leaving the transcript as the only substantive on-record account of committee thinking ahead of the July 28-29 meeting. CME FedWatch currently prices roughly a 73% chance the Fed holds rates steady this month.

Federal Reserve & Inflation

Reading the Minutes

A 9-9 Committee, a Chair Who Won’t Show His Hand, and an Oil Shock That Just Complicated Everything

The June minutes landed Wednesday afternoon into a market that was still digesting Tuesday's renewed strikes, and the timing mattered. Nine of eighteen officials favored at least one 2026 hike; nine did not; the document offered no tiebreaker because Chair Kevin Warsh declined to submit his own projection to the Summary of Economic Projections for the first time since the dot plot's 2012 debut. At the ECB's Sintra forum on July 1, Warsh was similarly noncommittal, telling attendees only that "we've seen that prices are too high" without projecting a path forward. What has changed since the meeting is the inflation backdrop itself: oil is now materially higher than it was on June 17, thanks to Tuesday's tanker strikes and Wednesday's escalation, undercutting the disinflationary narrative that had briefly taken hold after June's soft jobs report. Markets currently price a roughly 73% chance of a July hold via CME FedWatch, with September odds far more contested. The July 28-29 meeting, and whatever the oil market and the Iran conflict look like by then, will do more to settle the debate than Wednesday's transcript did.

U.S. Debt Crosses 100% of GDP for the First Time Since World War II — Armstrong Calls It a Crisis of Confidence, Not Cash

Armstrong Economics' latest posts, tagged War, Energy, and Sovereign Debt Crisis, converge on a single argument: this milestone is not merely symbolic. Unlike the postwar decline from 1946, there is today no peace dividend, no manufacturing boom, and no political appetite to cut spending — every election cycle promises more borrowing rather than less. Armstrong's framework holds that sovereign debt crises never begin with a sudden default; they unfold gradually through rising interest costs, capital migration, and an eroding confidence that governments can or will restore fiscal discipline, ultimately forcing higher taxes and tighter capital controls. His July interviews have specifically flagged a July-August escalation window tying the Iran conflict directly to this debt dynamic, arguing war spending and sanctions accelerate the same deficits driving the crisis. Tax & Accounting — Final Reminder

One Day Left: Kwong-Related Form 843 Protective Claims Are Due Tomorrow, July 10

Taxpayers who paid failure-to-file or failure-to-pay penalties on a 2019-2022 Form 1040 during the COVID disaster period have until tomorrow to file Form 843 and preserve their right to a refund under Kwong v. United States, now on appeal. As of this month, individual filers with an IRS Online Account can submit electronically for fully paid interest and penalties; businesses and anyone else must mail a paper form to the Ogden, UT service center, writing "Kwong vs. United States" across the top. National Taxpayer Advocate Erin Collins has publicly criticized the compressed timeline, calling a missed deadline “not a fair result” for taxpayers who may otherwise qualify. A separate Form 843 is required for each tax year.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

With Severe Storms Possible This Afternoon, a Quick Refresher on Basic Lightning and Flash-Flood Safety

Today's forecast — a heavy, potentially severe thunderstorm risk with hail and damaging winds — is a good prompt to review the basics that most heat-related storm injuries and fatalities trace back to. The National Weather Service's rule of thumb is simple: if you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck by lightning, so head indoors or into a hard-topped vehicle immediately rather than waiting to see rain. Once inside, stay off corded phones and away from plumbing and windows until 30 minutes after the last rumble. For anyone driving this afternoon, the more common killer is actually water: just six inches of fast-moving floodwater can knock over an adult, and roughly a foot can sweep away most vehicles, so turning around rather than driving through a flooded street is the safer call even if the route looks familiar.

Random Fact

The Imam Reza shrine complex in Mashhad, where Khamenei is being laid to rest today, is one of the largest mosque complexes on Earth — a sprawling arrangement of courtyards, prayer halls, museums, and libraries that can accommodate several hundred thousand worshippers at once during peak pilgrimage periods. Iranian tourism officials have estimated the site draws more annual visits than any single pilgrimage site outside Mecca, a reflection of how central Mashhad is to Shia religious life independent of today's political funeral.

Word of the Day
brinkmanship

(noun, mid-20th century, coined during the Cold War) — the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge of open conflict in order to extract concessions, on the theory that the other side will blink first. Tuesday's tanker strikes, Wednesday's "it's over," and Thursday's "we've already won militarily" read less like a fixed strategy than an improvised one — but brinkmanship is the word for it either way.
