Geopolitics · Iran · Saturday Edition

Trump Says Iran War Will End "Very Soon," Discloses 18 US Troops Killed, as a Weak Jobs Report Sends Stocks to Fresh Highs

President Trump said Friday that the Iran war would end "very soon," adding it would probably happen "right after the midterms" — a timeline that has become a recurring refrain as the conflict approaches its seventh month. In the same remarks, Trump disclosed that 18 U.S. service members have been killed over the course of the war, the first time the administration has attached a specific figure to American casualties.

Investigators still haven't established any connection between Iran and Wednesday's FlyDubai cockpit attack, in which a co-pilot stabbed the captain of an Israel-bound flight carrying more than 180 people before being subdued. Trump reiterated Friday that Tehran would be hit "very hard" if a link is ultimately confirmed, even as officials caution the inquiry remains open.

The domestic economic story competed for attention Friday after September's jobs report badly missed expectations: employers added just 29,000 payrolls, far below the 84,000 forecast, while the unemployment rate rose to 4.2%. July and August gains were also revised down by a combined 60,000 jobs, and average hourly earnings grew just 3% year-over-year — the slowest pace since 2021. The weak data cooled expectations for further Fed rate hikes and sent stocks sharply higher.

The Dow gained 250.40 points (+0.49%) to close at 51,176.96, the S&P 500 rose 0.73% to 7,722.72, and the Nasdaq surged 1.6% to a fresh all-time high, as the 10-year Treasury yield eased to roughly 5.27% from this week's 5.33%–5.34% multi-decade peak. Gold fell to around $4,140, down 0.9%, as a firmer dollar weighed on the metal, while Bitcoin held steady near $84,600 following Citigroup's fresh $113,000 price target. The 30-year mortgage rate held at 7.28%, with the next official Freddie Mac reading due Thursday.

A Promise Kept Open

A Threat That Doesn't Require Confirmation to Keep Working

Reiterating the "very hard" response before the FlyDubai investigation concludes lets the threat keep doing deterrent work in the meantime, regardless of whether the eventual findings point toward Iran or somewhere else entirely.

A Miss That Moved Markets

29,000 Jobs Is a Weak Number Wearing a Bullish Market Reaction

Stocks rallying on bad labor data only makes sense once you remember the rally is about the Fed path, not the economy itself — a reminder that "good news for stocks" and "good news for workers" have genuinely diverged this cycle.

Revisions Rarely Make Headlines

A Combined 60,000-Job Downgrade Is the Quietest Part of the Report

Revising two months of prior gains down by a combined 60,000 jobs suggests the labor market has been softer for longer than the real-time data initially showed, a pattern that tends to validate itself in subsequent revisions more often than not.

Geopolitics

The Deadline That Keeps Resetting

"Right After the Midterms" Has Become Its Own Kind of Milestone

Anchoring the war's resolution to an electoral calendar rather than a battlefield or negotiating condition means the deadline will keep sliding as long as the midterms remain in the future — which, for now, they still do.

An Unusual Disclosure

Naming a Casualty Figure Is Its Own Form of Political Signaling

Administrations disclose war casualties on their own terms far more often than they're forced to by outside reporting, and the timing of this one — alongside a fresh "very soon" promise — reads as an attempt to frame the cost as both real and nearly over.

Armstrong Economics

Armstrong: Casualty Disclosures Timed to Victory Narratives Are a Classic War Cycle Signature

Armstrong Economics has long observed that governments tend to reveal the human cost of a conflict precisely when they're also promising its imminent end, using the first disclosure to frame sacrifice as finite and nearly complete rather than open-ended. The site's War Cycle model treats Friday's pairing of the casualty figure with the "very soon" timeline as a textbook example of that pattern, regardless of whether the underlying timeline actually holds.

Health, Wellness & Fitness

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome Formally Renamed Under New Global Medical Consensus

Polycystic ovary syndrome has been officially renamed polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) under a global consensus published in The Lancet, backed by 56 medical organizations and informed by more than 14,000 survey responses from patients and clinicians. Advocates say the new name better reflects the condition's broad metabolic and hormonal impact beyond the ovaries, and may reduce the diagnostic delays and dismissiveness many patients have long reported under the old terminology.
