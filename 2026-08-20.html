Geopolitics · Markets · Thursday Edition

UAE Halts All Trade With Iran After Renewed Missile Fire as Tehran Seizes an Emirati Tanker Over Unpaid Fees and Trump Vows “Economic Warfare”

The UAE announced Wednesday that it has halted all trade, commercial exchanges, and financial transactions with Iran "until further notice," citing the renewed missile fire reported a day earlier as part of a pattern of "regional escalations that undermine regional and international peace and security." The UAE's own Ministry of Defense offered a more measured account of the incident itself, saying the two missiles it tracked fell harmlessly into the sea with no damage or casualties — while Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei went further still, flatly denying Tehran launched any missiles at all. The gap between an economic response this sweeping and an underlying incident this disputed is itself a data point: the UAE's patience for even ambiguous provocations appears to have run out.

Iran, for its part, spent the same news cycle demonstrating exactly how it intends to enforce the toll-and-permit framework floated in Monday's Oman-Iran joint statement. State media outlet Fars News Agency reported that Iranian forces seized a UAE-owned tanker near Qeshm Island days earlier, towing it into port after accusing the vessel of ignoring "the Iranian route" and failing to make "payment for services and Iran's permit." It's the first concrete case of Iran actually enforcing the fee-and-routing regime it and Oman had only just begun discussing as a framework — and gives the UAE's trade halt a much sharper edge than a purely rhetorical response to disputed missile fire.

Washington escalated on a second, entirely different front. President Trump declared flatly that "this will be Economic Warfare and Isolation on an unprecedented scale," calling it "the most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, elaborating on the same push, described the strategy as "a combination of economic isolation like the world has never seen before, and the continued blockade in the Strait of Hormuz." The concrete mechanism: Treasury said it would at least double its buybacks of long-dated 10-, 20-, and 30-year debt, from roughly $2 billion to at least $4 billion, explicitly aimed at pulling down long-end yields — a move that puts Bessent in visible tension with Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's own, more cautious approach to the same question.

Markets, notably, liked the yield relief far more than they feared the "economic warfare" rhetoric. Wednesday's cash session snapped a three-day losing streak (S&P 500 +0.2% to 7,707.98, Dow +0.2% to 53,463.05, Nasdaq +0.2% to 26,331.09), and Bitcoin ripped higher overnight to roughly $71,970 as investors read Treasury's buyback plan as a de facto liquidity injection. Oil climbed too — Brent above $94 and WTI above $88, both up roughly 3% — as the tanker seizure and trade halt added a fresh geopolitical premium on top of the yield story. Wednesday afternoon's FOMC minutes, from the contentious July 28–29 meeting, showed regional presidents Lorie Logan, Beth Hammack, and Neel Kashkari all dissenting in favor of a hike rather than a cut, a detail that reads almost like a footnote against the day's other headlines but underscores how divided the Fed remains even as the White House pursues its own extra-Fed levers on yields. Armstrong Economics says Thursday's news makes explicit what had only been implicit before: the economic campaign and the military one are now, functionally, a single war.

A Handful of Nuts a Day May Cut High Blood Pressure Risk by 20%

New research suggests that eating a daily handful of nuts — almonds, walnuts, or a mixed variety — is associated with roughly a 20% lower risk of developing high blood pressure over time. Researchers say the effect likely reflects a combination of nuts' unsaturated fats, fiber, magnesium, and potassium content, and note the finding fits a broader pattern in nutrition science where whole-food substitutions tend to outperform single-nutrient supplementation for long-term cardiovascular outcomes.
